\begin{abstract}
Large language models reach their users not as raw next-token predictors but as the products of an extensive post-training pipeline---instruction tuning, reward modelling, and preference optimisation---that installs the characteristic behaviours of a deployed assistant on top of a base model trained purely on web text. Yet mechanistic interpretability has concentrated overwhelmingly on base models, leaving the internal effects of post-training comparatively unexamined at the representational level. This thesis addresses that gap by asking a specific, empirically answerable version of a contested question in the alignment literature: at the feature level, does instruction tuning introduce new features into a language model's internal representations, or does it predominantly reorganise existing ones?

The question is operationalised using matched Sparse Autoencoders from the recent Gemma Scope 2 release, which for the first time makes publicly available directly comparable dictionaries for the pre-trained and instruction-tuned variants of a production-class language model (Gemma 3 1B). A four-stage pipeline is developed that collects residual-stream activations from both checkpoints over 5,000 FineWeb sequences, classifies features into preserved, modified, and variant-exclusive categories using decoder cosine similarity and activation correlation as complementary correspondence measures, attaches descriptive labels to a frequency-weighted sample of features through dual-LLM consensus annotation with inter-annotator reliability analysis, and cross-references the resulting classification against targeted differential activation tests on three behavioural probe datasets covering safety, instruction-following, and truthfulness.

The findings converge on a consistent descriptive picture. Cleanly preserved features are a small minority of the active feature set, and the features most behaviourally relevant to the characteristic prompts of an instruction-tuned model---instruction-shaped inputs and harmful content---are predominantly features already present and already selective in the base model, with instruction tuning amplifying and reweighting rather than introducing them. These findings provide the first feature-level evidence from a real instruction-tuned language model consistent with the ``wrapper hypothesis'' of Jain et al.\ (2024), previously tested only in synthetic settings, and motivate a reading of instruction tuning as a reorganisation of a pre-existing representational substrate rather than an installation of new computational structure---a framing with direct implications for how alignment teams should reason about the base-model origins of deployed safety behaviours.

\end{abstract}
