\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This chapter addresses the motivation in Subsection~\ref{sec:problem-motivation} and the research question from Subsubsection~\ref{subsubsec:gap-rq}. The chapter outlines what is concluded from the evidence, develops the implications of these conclusions for both interpretability methodologies and alignment practices, and identifies areas of potential future work. Subsection~\ref{subsec:conclusion-stated-at-supported-level} states the conclusions at the level of confidence the data supports. Subsection~\ref{subsec:return-to-motivation-and-rq} links the results back to the original framing questions. Subsection~\ref{subsec:implications-for-alignment-practice} draws out implications for alignment. Subsection~\ref{subsec:pipeline-as-methodological-contribution} frames the pipeline as a reusable methodological contribution. Finally, Subsection~\ref{subsec:future-work-directions} describes directions for future work, the most important of which is behaviourally grounded causal validation. Subsection~\ref{subsec:closing-reflection} offers a closing reflection.

\subsection{The Conclusion, Stated at the Level the Data Supports}
\label{subsec:conclusion-stated-at-supported-level}

Evidence from Chapter~\ref{sec:results} supports the following descriptive and correlational conclusions. In layer 13 of Gemma 3 1B, the behaviorally relevant instructional and safety features are mainly composed of reorganized pre-existing features instead of newly added ones. The evidence does not provide sufficient support for the stronger interpretation (i.e., that there exist no new features at all), since the IT-exclusive class is not empty, and a substantial proportion of the active IT features do not correspond to a strong PT-feature. However, the evidence does provide support for the weaker interpretation: the features responsible for the behavioral signature of instruction tuning---responding to instructions and to harmful content---reside within the modified class, which, by definition, contains elements that have a corresponding element in the base model. Therefore, the evidence supports the existence of the IT-exclusive class, but shows that this is not the site of behavioral activity.

The above conclusion is compatible with the wrapper hypothesis of \textcite{jain_mechanistically_2024}, originally presented in synthetic environments using pruning and linear probing. This study represents an extension of the wrapper hypothesis to a real-world instruction-tuned language model, at the feature level, based on SAEs as a descriptive vocabulary and on targeted differential analysis using three probe datasets instantiating two behavioral contrasts as the behavioral basis. The extension is not causal validation (see Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion}), but it constitutes a meaningful descriptive result connecting the Jain et al.\ proposal to a production-class model. The two results that carry the burden of supporting this conclusion are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the cross-model feature index overlap in Section~\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results} regarding the instruction-following contrast, where the top-ranked differential features in the IT model coincide with the top-ranked features in the PT model with large effect sizes in both models; and
    \item the distributional pattern of Sections~\ref{subsec:feature-classification-threshold-robustness}--\ref{subsec:automated-labelling-inter-annotator-agreement}, where instruction-type features are concentrated in the modified class instead of either the preserved or the IT-exclusive classes.
\end{itemize}

Finally, this conclusion is also compatible with the broader body of research concerning mechanistic fine-tuning since Jain et al.: \textcite{prakash_fine-tuning_2024} show that fine-tuning enhances existing mechanisms for entity tracking rather than introducing new ones, and the sparse-model-diffing literature has reached agreement on similar results in different model families.

\subsection{Return to the Motivation and the Research Question}
\label{subsec:return-to-motivation-and-rq}

The thesis started from an argument on if instruction tuning reorganises existing knowledge or creates genuinely new structure. The answer supported here is the weaker reorganisation claim: instruction tuning does not appear to create most of the features carrying the behavioural signatures of instruction-following and safety, even though the IT-exclusive class is not empty.

This supports one particular conclusion regarding the superficial alignment literature. \textcite{zhou_lima_2023}'s LIMA observation states that a relatively small amount of well-performing supervised training data can produce significant amounts of instructional behavior. This suggests that the base model contains at least some portion of the required substrate for this behavior. In addition, the feature-level analysis presented above aligns with this interpretation in ways previous research did not. Specifically, the selectivity of certain SAE feature indices in the base model for instruction-shaped prompts is clearly observable, and the IT model amplifies these selections. As such, features 2006 and 446, and the other features in the cross-model overlap set defined in Section~\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}, represent individualized representational objects that superficial alignment theory claims should exist, were demonstrated to exist in the current study, and can be identified individually.

In a similar manner, the safety comparison demonstrates a parallel, yet weaker, version of this story. As stated previously, the top-ranked features associated with harmful prompts are common across all checkpoint models. Also, none of the top twenty safety features of the IT model are unique to the IT model.

\subsection{Implications for Alignment Practice}
\label{subsec:implications-for-alignment-practice}

If the generalizable illustration of Section~\ref{subsec:conclusion-stated-at-supported-level} holds beyond the confines of this case study (a notion that this thesis supports but cannot verify), there are many consequences for how alignment will be practiced. These are discussed below as ``hypotheses''---not as ``claims supported by the data'' collected within this work.

\textbf{Base-model auditing becomes a meaningful safety intervention.} If instruction tuning is essentially just reorganizing the original characteristics of the model, then the original characteristic set of the base model provides a ceiling on what the instruction-tuned model can represent. A feature not present in the base model cannot be created by simply re-weighting; and similarly, any feature that is present in the base model (regardless of its dormancy or latency) could potentially be amplified when the model is fine-tuned. Thus, if an alignment group wishes to understand which behaviors a particular instruction-tuned model may produce, they have a rational basis to audit the base model's SAE feature space to determine which latent capabilities fine-tuning may expose. This represents a more narrow version of two concerns identified in prior literature \parencite{zou_universal_2023,hubinger_sleeper_2024}: one being that behaviors not typically produced by the IT model may still be recovered via adversarial prompts, and another representing a ``sleeper agent'' concern where safety training may not eliminate, but instead hide, dangerous behavior. Here, the feature-level data directly supports both versions at a very narrow scale: since all the capabilities fine-tuning works upon are in the base model, any type of adversarial prompting intended to bypass fine-tuning and thus elicit behavior from the base model itself would likely find behavior in the base model rather than some feature unique to instruction tuning.

\textbf{Safety fine-tuning is a reweighting, not a replacement.} The findings of Section~\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}, that most IT safety features were taken from the base model population, suggest that safety behavior is represented by modulating pre-existing features, not by adding some new structure specifically for safety in the process of fine-tuning. This conclusion aligns with observations made elsewhere \parencite{jain2024makesbreakssafetyfinetuning} that safety fine-tuning can be undone through further fine-tuning on seemingly unrelated tasks. Since we know safety is a re-weighting and not a structural addition, any additional training that alters those weights (whether or not done through adversarial processes) can plausibly degrade it. While the feature-level evidence presented does not provide proof for this mechanism, it is the type of evidence such a mechanism would predict. The results of Section~\ref{subsubsec:cross-sae-direction-robustness-check} offer somewhat weaker support for this idea, suggesting that SAE directions used across checkpoints are not treated entirely as random perturbations, although these results are sufficiently ambiguous to provide little support for any practically useful conclusions.

\subsection{The Pipeline as a Reusable Methodological Contribution}
\label{subsec:pipeline-as-methodological-contribution}

What stands out as the novel methodology is that the choice was made to use dual-LLM annotator disagreement as data (signal) instead of simply as error. Since the two annotators are concentratedly disagreeing on the \texttt{language\_syntax}~$\leftrightarrow$~\texttt{instruction\_style} border, the labelling process is doing more than assigning names to characteristics; it is giving us an empirical measurement of when our taxonomic system becomes ambiguous. This will be valuable for making use of this data to informally identify the boundaries of our taxonomical interpretation system using multiple annotators, and is therefore the portion of the pipeline we would like to carry with us to future studies comparing matched SAEs.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{subsec:future-work-directions}
\label{sec:future-work}

Six areas of potential direction for future research can be inferred from the findings and limitations of the paper, in descending order of their relevance.

\textbf{Behaviorally grounded causal validation.} The major disadvantage (Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion}) that has been found so far is the lack of an experimental design that tests whether the candidate features will indeed lead to the behaviors they have been selected for. A complete edition would steer each feature individually (for example, cross-modal instruction-style features 2006 and 446, or safety-related features 262 and 2184) and record specific task metrics: refusal rates on JailbreakBench when safety-related features are steered, or accuracy on Alpaca when instruction-style features are steered. The KL-based metric described in Section~\ref{subsubsec:cross-sae-direction-robustness-check}, however, is insufficient, since it records how much the distributions have changed but does not assess how those changes relate to the specific tasks being performed. We therefore suggest prioritizing this experiment over any other area of future work.

\textbf{Multilayer replication.} In Chapter~\ref{sec:results}, results were reported only at Layer~13. If the entire pipeline is rerun at every layer with identical sets of SAEs, one may obtain a view of how instruction tuning affects each layer. Such a layer profile may help determine whether instruction tuning affects the residual stream uniformly throughout the layers, concentrates at middle layers (i.e., layers with the highest level of semantic features), or at output layers (i.e., layers where behavioral decisions are taken).

\textbf{Cross-family replication.} The findings reported in this paper are based on a single case study. By running the same experiments on other model families once equivalent SAEs are available, researchers may verify whether the consistent view provided by the wrapper hypothesis holds outside of DeepMind's instruction tuning of Gemma~3 1B. Several plausible alternatives exist, including Llama~3, Mistral, and Qwen; the limiting factor is currently access to matched SAEs, not pipeline complexity.

\textbf{Safety prompts near boundaries.} If the same targeted differential analysis is run using prompts around the refusal boundary (i.e., prompts where minor wording changes result in compliance versus non-compliance), researchers may verify whether explicit safety features appear more prominently in the IT top lists than was observed in~\S\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}, using a stronger behavioral probe than a fixed harmful set.

\textbf{Cross-checkpoint crosscoders.} Instead of using two independent SAEs, a single crosscoder trained simultaneously on both PT and IT residual streams---similar to those used in the sparse-model-diffing literature---would eliminate the matched-SAE comparison problem by definition. Crosscoders were not employed in this research because the Gemma Scope~2 \parencite{mcdougall_gemma_scope_2_2025} crosscoders released are cross-layer rather than cross-checkpoint, and no public PT~$\leftrightarrow$~IT crosscoder existed. A future release would enable researchers to recompute the classification using a single common vocabulary derived from a shared dictionary, and comparing separate-SAE and crosscoder classifications would also be of methodological interest.

\textbf{Threshold sensitivity for active-feature fraction.} One minor unfinished piece: a formal sensitivity grid was not generated across active-feature thresholds. The classification proportions were informally checked and do not appear strongly sensitive within the 0.05\%--0.5\% range, but extending the grid in~\S\ref{subsec:threshold-selection} to three dimensions (including this threshold) would close this loose end.

\subsection{Closing Reflection}
\label{subsec:closing-reflection}

This dissertation was intended to test an argumentative issue regarding instruction tuning based on a feature-based pipeline, and it gives an illustrative description which matches the "wrapper" reading: at layer thirteen of Gemma 3 1B, the majority of the features most closely related to the instruction-following and safety prompt behaviours have undergone reorganisation of existing features and not primarily new ones. However, the answer given by the dissertation does not provide causal results, nor multi-layered results, nor multi-modelled results and therefore, is not the final result. Instead, it is a concrete, reproducible piece of information relative to the feature-based side of the current debate concerning instruction-tuning that has taken place largely at the behavioral and circuit levels and a pipeline that can be run again when matched SAEs will be available for other model-pairs. The original formulation of the wrapper hypothesis was developed for synthetic tasks and tested through pruning and probing; however, this dissertation extended its application to a production-class model that had been tuned through instructions and testing of the hypothesis with dictionary learning. In my opinion, the greatest contribution of this dissertation is that it represents a convergent outcome from two independently derived methods that have provided compatible descriptions. Therefore, the next logical steps (i.e., validating causality, replicating across multiple layers, and replicating across different families) represent a continued development along the same lines as those upon which this dissertation builds.
