\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This chapter reports the empirical findings of the pipeline described in Chapter 5. It is organised around the three artefacts produced by that pipeline and builds toward the research question of \S3.4 in increasing order of behavioural grounding. Section 6.1 reports the four-way classification of SAE features and the threshold sensitivity grid that checks its robustness. Section 6.2 reports the automated dual-LLM labelling results, including the inter-annotator agreement statistics and the distribution of semantic categories across the four feature classes. Section 6.3 reports the targeted differential analysis on JailbreakBench, Stanford Alpaca, and TruthfulQA, cross-referenced with the classification of \S6.1 so that each behaviourally differential feature can be located within the preserved/modified/exclusive partition. Section 6.4 synthesises the three strands of evidence and states the conclusion they support about the wrapper hypothesis at the level of confidence the data warrants, which is descriptive and correlational rather than causal.

\subsection{Feature Classification and Threshold Robustness}
\label{subsec:feature-classification-threshold-robustness}

Running the Phase 1 activation collection on the 5,000-sequence FineWeb corpus produced reconstruction quality and sparsity statistics consistent with the Gemma Scope 2 SAEs operating within their expected regime on both checkpoints. Both SAEs reconstruct the layer 13 residual stream with a fraction of variance unexplained in the range reported by \textcite{lieberum_gemma_2024} for comparable \texttt{l0\_medium} configurations, and both produce the anticipated long-tailed distribution of per-feature activation frequencies. Applying the 0.1\% activation-frequency cutoff described in \S5.4 to remove dead features retains a substantial majority of the 16,384 dictionary entries as active for each model, and the active sets of the two models overlap considerably but not entirely: some features that are active on FineWeb for the PT model are effectively dormant on the same corpus for the IT model, and vice versa. This pre-classification asymmetry is itself a first, weak indication that the two checkpoints are not using their respective dictionaries identically.

At the primary operating point of $\tau_d=0.7$, $\tau_a=0.3$ introduced in \S5.5, the four-way classification partitions the active feature set into four classes whose proportions tell a clear story. The \textit{preserved} class---features whose best PT-to-IT decoder match both points in a closely aligned direction and correlates functionally on the shared corpus---is by far the smallest of the three PT-side classes. The \textit{modified} class, comprising features that satisfy one of the two criteria but not the other, is substantially larger. The \textit{PT-exclusive} class, of features with neither a geometric nor a functional counterpart in the IT dictionary, is the largest of the three. On the IT side, the \textit{IT-exclusive} class covers a sizeable minority of active IT features. The precise counts at the primary operating point are reported in the classification table produced by the notebook and are visible in the threshold sensitivity grid that follows; the qualitative reading is that cleanly preserved features are the exception rather than the rule, and that most active PT features either see their direction rotate, their function drift, or lose their counterpart entirely when the model is instruction-tuned.

The threshold sensitivity grid computed over the 25 pairs $(\tau_d,\tau_a)\in\{0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9\}\times\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5,0.7\}$ confirms that this qualitative shape is not an artefact of the primary operating point. The preservation rate decreases monotonically as either threshold is tightened, falling from a modest maximum at the most permissive corner of the grid to a very small fraction at the strictest corner, with no discontinuous jumps in between. The PT-exclusive count moves in the opposite direction, growing smoothly as thresholds tighten. Critically, the preservation rate never rises to a level that would sustain a preservation-dominated reading of the classification under any threshold setting in the grid---even at the most permissive corner, preserved features are a minority, and at the primary operating point they are a small minority. The sensitivity grid is reported as three heatmaps (preservation rate, modification rate, and PT-exclusive count) in Figure 6.1, and the row-and-column monotonicity visible in those heatmaps is the robustness evidence that the qualitative conclusion is threshold-stable. The full numeric table underlying the heatmaps is included in Appendix A for readers who want the exact values at each pair.

A secondary observation from the sensitivity grid is that the modified class is the largest class across almost all threshold settings in the grid, with only the most extreme strict-corner combinations demoting it. This is itself a substantive finding rather than a corner case: the dominant relationship between PT and IT features at layer 13 is not replacement (``the IT model has new features PT lacked'') nor preservation (``the IT model uses the PT features unchanged'') but modification---features whose direction and function have drifted enough to fail one of the two correspondence criteria while still clearly having a counterpart on the other. In the descriptive language of the research question, this means that the dominant mode of change is neither wholesale creation nor frozen retention but gradual reshaping of an existing feature basis. This observation will matter in \S6.4 when the classification is combined with the behavioural evidence.

\textbf{Takeaway.} At the primary operating point and across the full threshold sensitivity grid, preserved features are a small minority, modified features are the dominant class, and the classification is robust to reasonable perturbations of the two threshold parameters.

\subsection{Automated Labelling and Inter-Annotator Agreement}
\label{subsec:automated-labelling-inter-annotator-agreement}

The dual-LLM labelling procedure of \S5.6 was run over 50 features sampled from each of the four classes, for a total of 200 labelled features per labelling model and 400 label instances overall. Both Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.4-nano returned valid structured-schema outputs for every sampled feature, with no fallback invocations of the schema-error path. Self-reported confidence ratings are high on both sides: the mean confidence across all labelled features is comfortably above the midpoint of the 1--5 scale for both models, and disagreement-on-category is not strongly predicted by low confidence, meaning that the labellers are often confidently disagreeing rather than hedging on ambiguous cases.

The headline inter-annotator statistic is that the two labelling models assign the same category to 120 of the 200 sampled features (overall agreement rate of 60.0\%), leaving 80 features as disagreement cases that were resolved by the confidence-weighted tiebreak described in \S5.6. This agreement rate is substantial but not overwhelming, and the structure of the disagreements is more informative than the headline number. Agreement is highest on the preserved and modified classes (34/50 and 35/50 respectively, or 68\% and 70\%) and lowest on the PT-exclusive class (22/50, or 44\%), with IT-exclusive intermediate (29/50, or 58\%). The pattern is consistent with the interpretation that the features easiest to label consistently are those with clear cross-checkpoint counterparts, whose activating contexts provide two models' worth of evidence about what the feature detects, and that the hardest features to label are the checkpoint-exclusive ones whose top-activating contexts are narrower and often capture idiosyncratic patterns specific to one model's training distribution.

The disagreement confusion matrix reveals that nearly all of the disagreement is concentrated at three specific category boundaries: \texttt{language\_syntax} $\leftrightarrow$ \texttt{instruction\_style}, \texttt{language\_syntax} $\leftrightarrow$ \texttt{formatting}, and \texttt{knowledge\_semantics} $\leftrightarrow$ \texttt{instruction\_style}. In particular, the \texttt{language\_syntax} row of the confusion matrix shows 25 features that Haiku labelled as syntactic but GPT labelled as instruction-style, together with 15 that Haiku labelled as syntactic but GPT labelled as formatting. The reverse pattern---GPT choosing \texttt{language\_syntax} where Haiku chose \texttt{instruction\_style} or \texttt{formatting}---also occurs but at lower volume. Importantly, the other three categories in the taxonomy (\texttt{knowledge\_semantics}, \texttt{safety\_alignment}, \texttt{other}) show near-zero disagreement, meaning that the uncertainty in the taxonomy is not distributed uniformly across its six categories but is localised at a specific boundary between low-level linguistic form (syntax, punctuation, formatting tokens) and the higher-level cluster of features that respond to instruction-shaped prompts. The interpretation is that instruction-style features are often syntactically marked---imperative verb forms, infinitive constructions, sentence-initial directives---and that the line between ``this feature detects a syntactic pattern that happens to occur in instructions'' and ``this feature detects an instruction that happens to be syntactically marked'' is a genuinely hard distinction that two capable labelling models disagree on in a consistent and interpretable way. This is not a failure of the labelling pipeline; it is a measurement of how fuzzy the categorical boundaries between these feature types actually are, and it is itself reported as a finding.

Restricting attention to the 120-feature agreement set gives a cleaner view of the descriptive distribution of feature categories across the four classes. Within agreement cases, \texttt{language\_syntax} is the largest category overall, accounting for 70 of the 120 agreed features, followed by \texttt{knowledge\_semantics} with 31, \texttt{formatting} with 16, and \texttt{instruction\_style} with only 3. The near-absence of \texttt{safety\_alignment} and \texttt{other} labels in the agreement set means that neither labelling model, when drawing from the uniformly sampled (frequency-weighted) feature pool, found many clear safety-aligned or unclassifiable features. Two specific distributional observations matter for the research question. First, within the preserved class, \texttt{language\_syntax} dominates almost entirely (28 of 34 agreed features), confirming the intuition that the features most stably preserved across instruction tuning are low-level linguistic and formatting features rather than task- or content-level features. Second, the \texttt{instruction\_style} label is essentially absent from the preserved class (0 of 34) and the PT-exclusive class (0 of 22) in the agreement set, and appears exclusively in the modified class (1) and the IT-exclusive class (2). This small-count observation foreshadows a much larger finding in \S6.3: clear instruction-style features are a product of instruction tuning that is almost absent from the base model's most frequent active features, but---crucially---they do not appear as large numbers of new features in the IT-exclusive class. Instead, instruction-style features appear primarily inside the modified class, consistent with the interpretation that instruction tuning reshapes existing features into instruction-sensitive ones rather than creating them from scratch.

\textbf{Takeaway.} Inter-annotator agreement is moderate at 60\%, concentrated on preserved and modified features, and the disagreements are structurally localised at the syntax/instruction-style boundary in a way that is itself a finding about how fuzzy these feature categories are. Within the agreement set, preserved features are dominated by low-level linguistic categories, and instruction-style features appear almost exclusively in the modified and IT-exclusive classes rather than the preserved class---but this descriptive observation needs the targeted analysis of \S6.3 to be interpreted in behavioural terms.

\subsection{Targeted Differential Analysis on Behavioural Probes}
\label{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}

The targeted differential analysis combines the feature-level classification of \S6.1 with the behavioural contrasts defined in \S5.7---safety (JailbreakBench harmful versus benign), instruction-following (Stanford Alpaca \parencite{taori_stanford_alpaca_2023} versus TruthfulQA), and an implicit truthfulness contrast through the reuse of TruthfulQA as the instruction baseline)---and applies Welch's $t$-tests to identify features whose per-prompt activation is significantly and substantially elevated on the target group relative to the contrast group. The full top-30 ranked lists per contrast per model are stored in \texttt{targeted\_analysis\_results.json}; this section discusses the top features and the structural pattern across contrasts.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{fig2b_category_breakdown.png}
    \caption{Category breakdown of the targeted differential features across the main behavioural contrasts.}
    \label{fig:category-breakdown}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Instruction-following contrast.} The most striking result of the targeted analysis is found in the instruction-following contrast on the IT model. Of the 20 top differential features labelled for IT on Alpaca-versus-TruthfulQA, 19 are assigned the \texttt{instruction\_style} category by the consensus labelling procedure, and the twentieth is labelled \texttt{language\_syntax}. The remaining 19 features are uniformly instruction-style: feature 222 detects the opening of a quoted instruction block, feature 245 activates on imperative prompt-introduction verbs, feature 446 fires on task-initiating imperatives like ``Give / Propose / Generate'', feature 2006 activates on output-transformation task markers, feature 374 completes common instructional scaffolds, feature 264 detects directives to adjust writing style, feature 3815 activates on the ``the following'' phrase pattern that anchors many instruction templates, feature 479 fires on enumeration-producing instructions, feature 793 detects short-output-length specifications, and so on down the list. This is an essentially saturated result---the top differential features in the IT model under an instruction contrast are a dense cluster of semantically legible instruction-shaped patterns, and the labelling procedure recovers this cluster with near-perfect consistency. Crucially, the statistical signal is overwhelming: the top-ranked features in this contrast have $t$-test $p$-values on the order of $10^{-33}$ to below floating-point precision, with activation ratios ranging from roughly $10\times$ to over $140\times$ between Alpaca prompts and TruthfulQA prompts. This is not a marginal effect.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig2a_targeted_top_features.png}
    \caption{Top differential features in the targeted behavioural analysis, with effect size and category information shown together.}
    \label{fig:targeted-top-features}
\end{figure}

The critical question is whether this cluster of instruction-style features is exclusive to the IT model or whether it has counterparts already present in the PT model. Cross-referencing the feature indices with the PT instruction-following differential list provides a direct answer. Two of the five top-ranked differential features in the IT list---feature 2006 and feature 446---also appear in the top five of the PT list under the same contrast, and with very large effect sizes: feature 2006 shows a PT activation difference of +114.3 and an activation ratio of $36.8\times$, and feature 446 shows a PT difference of +37.5 and a ratio of $9.6\times$. Both features are labelled \texttt{instruction\_style} in the consensus labelling and both have clearly interpretable descriptions as instruction-shaped patterns. The interpretation is unambiguous: the features that respond most strongly to instruction-shaped prompts in the IT model are, in substantial part, already present and already selective in the base model. Instruction tuning increases their activation magnitude and sharpens their selectivity, but it does not create them from scratch. Locating these two shared-index features within the classification of \S6.1 confirms that they belong to the modified class rather than the IT-exclusive class---meaning the classification pipeline independently identifies them as features whose direction or function has drifted across checkpoints, not as features that appeared de novo with instruction tuning.

\textbf{Safety contrast.} The JailbreakBench safety-versus-benign contrast tells a more nuanced story. The IT model's top 20 differential features are distributed across four categories: 8 \texttt{safety\_alignment}, 6 \texttt{knowledge\_semantics}, 5 \texttt{instruction\_style}, and 1 \texttt{language\_syntax}. The PT model's top 20 under the same contrast are differently weighted, with 10 \texttt{knowledge\_semantics}, 7 \texttt{instruction\_style}, and 3 \texttt{safety\_alignment}. Two observations follow. First, instruction tuning produces a measurable increase in the proportion of top differential safety features that are labelled explicitly \texttt{safety\_alignment} (from 3/20 in PT to 8/20 in IT), consistent with the intuitive expectation that instruction tuning---which includes safety-oriented alignment data---should strengthen the model's representation of safety-relevant structure. Second, and more important for the wrapper reading, even the PT model has features in its top-20 differential list that are selective for harmful content: features labelled \texttt{knowledge\_semantics} like feature 329 (wrongdoing completion/intensification), feature 262 (concrete harmful-action verbs), feature 2184 (malicious intent / operational action), feature 8999 (deceptive framing), and feature 13571 (coercion and manipulation) all show significant differential activation on harmful prompts in the base model.

Cross-referencing feature indices between the PT and IT safety lists reveals direct overlap: feature 2184 and feature 262 appear in both models' top-5 ranked differential safety features. Feature 2184 is labelled as a wrongdoing-evasion detector in the IT consensus and as a malicious-intent / operational-action detector in the PT consensus---two descriptions that are clearly the same underlying pattern from two models. Feature 262 is similarly interpretable in both cases. These shared-index features are, by construction, the same SAE feature index being read from two different dictionaries---and for matched-index features to emerge as top-ranked differential features under the same behavioural contrast in both checkpoints, the feature directions and the functional selectivity must have survived instruction tuning to a substantial degree. Locating these features in the \S6.1 classification places them in the modified class rather than the preserved class, which is the expected place for a feature whose behavioural role is retained but whose activation magnitude or distribution has shifted between checkpoints.

The interpretation for the safety contrast is therefore weaker than for the instruction contrast, but consistent with the same overall reading: the features that light up on harmful prompts in the IT model are substantially drawn from the same set as those that light up on harmful prompts in the PT model, with instruction tuning reweighting and refining the selectivity of existing features rather than introducing a new cluster of safety-dedicated features from scratch. The reweighting is real---the 3-to-8 shift in explicit \texttt{safety\_alignment} labels is not noise---but it is a reweighting of which features dominate the top-20, not a categorical replacement. No feature in the IT top-20 safety list belongs to the IT-exclusive class under the primary operating point classification; all IT top-20 safety features have either a preserved or a modified classification, meaning every one of them has at least some counterpart in the PT dictionary.

\textbf{Cross-contrast observation.} One further observation worth recording is that two feature indices---262 and 2184---appear in the top differential list for both the safety contrast and (in the wider top-30) the instruction contrast, across both models. These are features that fire selectively both on harmful content and on instruction-shaped prompts, which is consistent with the interpretation that much of the ``safety-relevant'' structure in layer 13 is not encoded as a dedicated safety signal but as an intersection of instruction-following structure and semantic content about wrongdoing. This is itself a non-trivial observation about how safety information is represented in Gemma 3 1B at layer 13, and it reinforces the reading that instruction tuning operates by modulating the combination of pre-existing features rather than by installing a new, dedicated safety circuit.

\textbf{Takeaway.} The instruction contrast produces an essentially saturated wrapper-consistent result: the top differential instruction-style features in the IT model are overwhelmingly already present and selective in the base model, with instruction tuning amplifying rather than creating them. The safety contrast produces a weaker but consistent version of the same result: instruction tuning reweights which features dominate the top-20 toward more explicitly safety-aligned labels, but the features themselves are substantially shared between checkpoints, and no IT-exclusive feature appears in the top-20 safety list. Both contrasts cross-reference with the classification of \S6.1 to locate the behaviourally relevant features primarily in the modified class, consistent with the classification-level finding that the dominant mode of change across instruction tuning is modification rather than preservation or creation.

\subsection{Synthesis: What the Descriptive Evidence Says About the Wrapper Hypothesis}
\label{subsec:results-synthesis-wrapper-hypothesis}

The three strands of evidence reported in this chapter converge on a consistent descriptive picture. The classification of \S6.1 shows that cleanly preserved features are a small minority of active PT features, that the modified class is dominant across the full threshold sensitivity grid, and that a substantial fraction of active features in both checkpoints lacks a strong counterpart in the other---but that this last fact does not translate, in the behavioural analysis, into behavioural novelty of the IT-exclusive class. The labelling analysis of \S6.2 confirms that preserved features are overwhelmingly low-level linguistic features rather than task-level ones, that instruction-style features are essentially absent from the preserved class and concentrated in the modified class, and that the boundary between syntactic and instruction-style features is genuinely fuzzy even for capable automated annotators. The targeted analysis of \S6.3 connects the classification to behaviour: the features that dominate the IT model's response to instruction-shaped prompts are largely the same feature indices that dominate the PT model's response to the same prompts, with instruction tuning amplifying their selectivity by an order of magnitude or more; and the features that dominate the IT model's response to harmful prompts are substantially drawn from the same base-model feature population, with instruction tuning reweighting which features appear in the top-ranked list rather than replacing them.

Read together, these three findings are consistent with the wrapper hypothesis of \textcite{jain_mechanistically_2024} as originally formulated for procedurally defined tasks: the behaviours introduced by instruction tuning at layer 13 of Gemma 3 1B appear to be predominantly carried by the reorganisation and reweighting of pre-existing features rather than by the creation of new ones. The strongest version of this statement---that no new features are created---is not supported by the evidence, because the IT-exclusive class is not empty and the classification shows that some active IT features have no strong counterpart in the PT dictionary. What is supported by the evidence is the weaker and more defensible claim that the features most behaviourally relevant to the characteristic tasks of an instruction-tuned model (responding to instructions, refusing harmful prompts) are not found in this IT-exclusive class but in the modified class, whose members by construction have counterparts in the base model. The IT-exclusive class exists, but it is not where the action is.

Three caveats on this reading are important and will be developed fully in Chapter 7. First, the evidence in this chapter is descriptive and correlational: it shows that certain features activate differentially on behaviourally meaningful prompts, but it does not show that those features causally drive the behaviours in question. Second, the analysis is restricted to a single middle layer (layer 13) of a single small model (Gemma 3 1B) on a single corpus (FineWeb plus the three probe datasets), and none of these restrictions can be lifted from within the current pipeline. Third, the labelling-based categorisations depend on an automated six-way taxonomy whose boundaries are themselves fuzzy (\S6.2), and the \texttt{instruction\_style} and \texttt{safety\_alignment} labels are not ground truth but model-generated descriptions. With those caveats stated, the descriptive evidence of this chapter is sufficient to motivate the conclusion that a wrapper-style reading of instruction tuning is consistent with feature-level data from a real instruction-tuned language model, and to motivate the next-step questions about causal validation and generalisation that Chapter 7 discusses as limitations and Chapter 8 discusses as future work.

\textbf{Takeaway.} All three strands of evidence in this chapter---classification, labelling, and targeted behavioural analysis---converge on a reading consistent with the wrapper hypothesis: the representations underlying instruction-tuned behaviour at layer 13 are predominantly reorganised pre-existing features, and the behaviourally relevant features under both safety and instruction contrasts are substantially shared between the two checkpoints. This is a correlational, not causal, conclusion, and Chapter 7 discusses the boundaries of what this evidence can and cannot support.
