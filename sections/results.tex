\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The current chapter provides an overview of the results from the pipeline discussed in Chap.~\ref{sec:methodology}. The chapter will report on the artefacts of the pipeline in increasing order of their behavioral relevance. In Sect.~\ref{subsec:feature-classification-threshold-robustness}, we describe our four-way classification and its sensitivity to thresholds. In Sect.~\ref{subsec:automated-labelling-inter-annotator-agreement}, we present the results of our dual-LLM labeling tasks along with measures of inter-annotator agreement and the distributions of semantic categories over feature classes. Sect.~\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results} presents the results of a targeted differential analysis based on JailbreakBench, Stanford Alpaca, and TruthfulQA (as the factual baseline for the instruction contrast) for two behavioral comparisons, referenced to Sect.~\ref{subsec:feature-classification-threshold-robustness} where each behaviorally differentiating feature can be identified among those preserved/modified/exclusive. Finally, in Sect.~\ref{subsec:results-synthesis-wrapper-hypothesis} we synthesize these three lines of evidence to tell us how much they collectively support the wrapper hypothesis.

\subsection{Feature Classification and Threshold Robustness}
\label{subsec:feature-classification-threshold-robustness}

In addition to achieving quality and sparsity characteristics (in terms of reconstructed data and number of non-zero values) consistent with those described by \parencite{mcdougall_gemma_scope_2_2025} for the Gemma Scope~2 SAEs operating at the expected regime (on both checkpoints), the 5,000-sequence FineWeb corpus also provided results for both SAEs which reconstruct the layer 13 residual stream with an FVUE in the same range as that reported by \textcite{lieberum_gemma_2024} for similar \texttt{l0\_medium} configurations on Gemma~2, and similarly long-tailed distributions for per-feature activation frequency. Using the 0.1\% activation-frequency cut-off defined by Subsection~\ref{subsec:feature-matching-classification} retained a large percentage of the 16,384 total dictionary entries as being "active" for each model; however, the two models did not use identical subsets of their dictionaries as active. In fact, while there was considerable overlap between the active sets for each model, there were many features that were considered active for the PT model that were essentially inactive for the IT model (and vice-versa). This degree of asymmetry is itself a second weak indicator that the two checkpoints do not have the same dictionaries.

The classification of the active subset of the PT features at the primary operating point ($\tau_d=0.7$, $\tau_a=0.3$) resulted in four distinct categories. Of the PT features identified as active, 241 were classified as \textit{preserved}, 4,017 as \textit{modified}, and 3,884 as \textit{PT-exclusive}. For the IT model, among its active features, 1,899 were classified as \textit{IT-exclusive}. The \textit{preserved} category is clearly the smallest of the three categories. The \textit{modified} category contains features where either the geometric and/or functional correspondence existed, but not both. The \textit{PT-exclusive} category includes all features that had no corresponding geometric and/or functional counterparts in the IT dictionary. The \textit{IT-exclusive} category included all the IT features with no geometric and/or functional correspondents in the PT dictionary. It should be clear from the threshold sensitivity grid below that these patterns are not specific to the choice of any single threshold value.

The primary operating point was selected such that its use could be extended by testing a set of five different thresholds for each of the two parameter $\tau_d$ and $\tau_a$, resulting in a total of 25 pairs: $(\tau_d,\tau_a)\in\{0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9\}\times\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5,0.7\}$. In summary, the results confirm that the results are not artifacts of how the original models were used. As both the $\tau_d$ and $\tau_a$ parameters become stricter, the preservation ratio continues to decrease; there are no abrupt changes in the preservation ratios as either parameter becomes stricter. The PT-only counts continue to increase in a smooth manner. At none of the thresholds will the preservation ratio rise to a sufficient value that will result in a preservation-dominant interpretation. Even when using the least stringent thresholds, preserved features represent less than half of the number of features considered preserved and/or modified. A figure illustrating the three heat maps representing preservation ratio, modification ratio, and PT-only counts are provided as Fig.\ref{fig:threshold-heatmaps}. The horizontal/vertical monotonicity exhibited within these heat maps provides additional robustness evidence.

A second observation is that nearly all threshold values result in a class size larger than that of both the PT and IT classes. Only extremely conservative corner combinations resulted in smaller sizes for the modified class. The overall relationship between PT and IT features at layer 13 is neither replacement ("IT includes new features not available in PT") nor preservation ("IT uses PT's same features"). Instead, the relationship is dominated by modification ("PT and IT have similar features that differ only in terms of their direction/function"). In addition, the majority of modifications are relatively gradual adjustments to an existing set of features. These observations regarding the nature of modifications will be important to consider in conjunction with the behavioral evidence in Subsection~\ref{subsec:results-synthesis-wrapper-hypothesis}.

Therefore, preserved features make up a minority of the preserved and modified classes across all thresholds at both the initial operating point and during the subsequent exploration of threshold sensitivities.

\subsection{Automated Labelling and Inter-Annotator Agreement}
\label{subsec:automated-labelling-inter-annotator-agreement}

The automated labelling process described in Subsection~\ref{subsec:automated-labelling} was applied to 50 features from each of the four categories, resulting in a total of 200 labelled features and 400 labelled feature occurrences. Both Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.4-nano produced valid output according to the structured-schema format for each sample feature. Confidence ratings reported by the labellers are very high for both models. For example, the average confidence rating for both models exceeds the middle point of the 1 -- 5 scale; there is little relationship between the degree of confidence in a label assignment and whether it agrees with another labeller.

Further, when the two models assign different categories to a feature, this does not usually reflect that either labeller is unsure about which category to use. Rather, the two labellers will usually have strong beliefs about what category is correct, even though they will not agree on which category. There were 120 instances out of 200 where the two models assigned the same category to a given feature (i.e., 60.0\% agreement), and therefore there were 80 instances of category disagreement that had been resolved by using the confidence-weighted tie-breaker provided in Subsection~\ref{subsec:automated-labelling}. While the structure of category disagreements provides considerably more information than the headline figure of "disagreement", we can report some general statistics. The category agreements are highest for preserved (68\%) and modified (70\%) classes and lowest for PT-exclusive (44\%) classes. In contrast, the category agreement rates for IT-exclusive classes fall somewhere in-between (58\%). Features that tend to be easy to label consistently are those that clearly map across checkpoints, whose activating contexts provide each model with evidence about what type of thing the feature detects. On the other hand, checkpoint-exclusionary features whose top-activating contexts are typically narrower and sometimes include idiosyncratic patterns particular to one model's training data are harder to label consistently.

As shown in the confusion matrix of disagreements, nearly all of the disagreements concentrate at just three category boundaries: \texttt{language\_syntax} $\leftrightarrow$ \texttt{instruction\_style}, \texttt{language\_syntax} $\leftrightarrow$ \texttt{formatting}, and \texttt{knowledge\_semantics} $\leftrightarrow$ \texttt{instruction\_style}. Of the 40 features labelled by Haiku as syntactic but by GPT as instruction-style, 25 were classified in this manner. Similarly, there were 15 such examples among the 40 features labelled by Haiku as syntactic but by GPT as formatting. The reverse pattern (where GPT chooses \texttt{language\_syntax} while Haiku chooses \texttt{instruction\_style} or \texttt{formatting}) occurred at somewhat lower volumes. The remaining three categories (\texttt{knowledge\_semantics}, \texttt{safety\_alignment}, and \texttt{other}) showed near-zero levels of disagreement. As noted earlier, the uncertainty in classification taxonomy is not uniform but localised at the boundary separating low-level linguistic form (syntax, punctuation, formatting tokens) and features that respond to instruction-shaped prompts. The explanation for this localisation is simple: instruction-style features are frequently syntactically marked (e.g., imperative verb forms, infinitive constructions, sentence-initial directives); therefore, the distinction between ``the feature detects a syntactic pattern that coincidentally appears in instructions'' and ``the feature detects an instruction that has a syntactically marked construction'' is difficult. This localisation is not due to a pipeline error but is instead indicative of the fuzziness in actual categorical boundaries; thus, it represents a finding.

The 120-feature agreement set allows for an easier overview of categorization distributions among all classes. Overall \texttt{language\_syntax} is the largest categorized group (70 of 120), followed by \texttt{knowledge\_semantics} (31), \texttt{formatting} (16), and lastly \texttt{instruction\_style} (3). \texttt{safety\_alignment} and \texttt{other} both had very few instances because there were so few clearly safety-aligned or non-classified features identified from the frequency-weighted feature pool used by either model. There are two important distribution-related findings. Firstly, within the preserved class, \texttt{language\_syntax} is dominant in almost all cases (28 of 34 agreed upon features) providing further evidence that the instruction-tuned features most consistently preserved across models are low-level linguistic and formatting characteristics not higher level content-based. Secondly, \texttt{instruction\_style} was absent from both the preserved class (0 of 34) and PT-exclusive class (0 of 22) in the agreement set, yet appeared only in the modified class (1) and IT-exclusive class (2) although it did have a small number of counts. The limited count of \texttt{instruction\_style} in this case foreshadowed a much greater discovery in Subsection~\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}. Instruction-style features are produced through instruction tuning to a degree largely absent from the base-models' most frequently active features; however, instruction-style features did not appear as large numbers of new features in the IT-exclusive class. Rather, they appeared mainly within the modified class and therefore, consistent with instruction tuning modifying pre-existing features into instruction-sensitive versions instead of producing them from the beginning.

In summary: There was an acceptable level of agreement among annotators (inter-annotator agreement) for preserved and modified feature types at 60\%. The disagreement is found to be located at the syntactic/instructional style boundaries; this too is a finding as it shows that the categorization of these two items is fuzzy. In addition, within the preserved feature type category there were primarily lower level linguistic categories. Instruction-type features were nearly all present in the modified and IT-exclusive classes.

\subsection{Targeted Differential Analysis on Behavioural Probes}
\label{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}

The targeted differential analysis brings together the feature classifications described in Subsection~\ref{subsec:feature-classification-threshold-robustness} with the two behavioral contrasts described in Subsection~\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis}: safety (JailbreakBench harmful vs. JailbreakBench benign), and instruction-following, which compares Stanford Alpaca \parencite{taori_stanford_alpaca_2023} with TruthfulQA, where TruthfulQA serves as the factual-question baseline. Welch's $t$-tests identify features that have significantly and substantially higher per prompt activation rates within the target group compared to the contrast group. The full top 30 ranked lists per contrast per model can be found in \texttt{targeted\_analysis\_results.json}; this section will discuss the most important features identified through these analyses and how they exhibit similar patterns across both contrasts.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{fig2b_category_breakdown.png}
    \caption{Category breakdown of the targeted differential features across the main behavioural contrasts.}
    \label{fig:category-breakdown}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Instruction-following contrast.} A very strong instruction-following contrast is present as well in the Instruction-Tuned (IT) model. Nineteen of the twenty top differential features that were labelled for IT on Alpaca-versus-TruthfulQA received the \texttt{instruction\_style} category from the consensus procedure; the 20th was \texttt{language\_syntax}. All nineteen instruction-style features make semantic sense. For example, feature 222 identifies when a quoted instruction block is opened, feature 245 is activated by the use of imperative prompt introduction words, feature 446 is fired by the use of task-initiating imperatives such as ``give'', ``propose'', or ``generate'', feature 2006 is activated by the task marker indicating that an output transformation task should be performed, feature 374 finishes off typical instructional scaffolding, feature 264 finds directive words to alter the way one writes, feature 3815 uses the pattern of the phrase ``the following'' as an anchor to many instruction templates, feature 479 fires upon receiving an enumeration-producing instruction, feature 793 finds short length specification of outputs, etc. There is essentially no ambiguity in this result. The results of the top differential features using an instruction contrast form a tight, semantically interpretable group, and the consensus labelling procedure recovered them with almost perfect consistency. The statistical signal here is overwhelmingly strong. Features that ranked at the top had $t$-test $p$-values ranging from around $10^{-33}$ down to below floating point precision, and there were activation ratios between Alpaca and TruthfulQA prompts ranging from just about $10\times$ up through more than $140\times$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig2a_targeted_top_features.png}
    \caption{Top differential features in the targeted behavioural analysis, with effect size and category information shown together.}
    \label{fig:targeted-top-features}
\end{figure}

The question is whether this cluster is exclusive to the IT model or has counterparts in the PT model. Cross-referencing feature indices with the PT instruction-following differential list gives a direct answer. Two of the five top-ranked IT features (feature 2006 and feature 446) also appear in the top five of the PT list under the same contrast, with large effect sizes: feature 2006 shows a PT activation difference of +114.3 and a ratio of $36.8\times$; feature 446 shows a PT difference of +37.5 and a ratio of $9.6\times$. Both are labelled \texttt{instruction\_style} in the consensus and have clearly interpretable descriptions as instruction-shaped patterns. The interpretation is clear: the features that respond most strongly to instruction-shaped prompts in the IT model are, in substantial part, already present and selective in the base model. Instruction tuning increases their activation magnitude and sharpens their selectivity but does not create them. Locating these shared-index features in the Subsection~\ref{subsec:feature-classification-threshold-robustness} classification confirms that they belong to the modified class, not the IT-exclusive class: the pipeline independently identifies them as features whose direction or function has drifted, not features that appeared de novo.

\textbf{Safety contrast.} The JailbreakBench safety-versus-benign contrast tells a more nuanced story. The IT model's top 20 differential features are distributed across four categories: 8 \texttt{safety\_alignment}, 6 \texttt{knowledge\_semantics}, 5 \texttt{instruction\_style}, and 1 \texttt{language\_syntax}. The PT model's top 20 under the same contrast are differently weighted, with 10 \texttt{knowledge\_semantics}, 7 \texttt{instruction\_style}, and 3 \texttt{safety\_alignment}. Two observations follow. First, instruction tuning produces a measurable increase in the proportion of top differential safety features that are labelled explicitly \texttt{safety\_alignment} (from 3/20 in PT to 8/20 in IT), consistent with the intuitive expectation that instruction tuning---which includes safety-oriented alignment data---should strengthen the model's representation of safety-relevant structure. Second, and more important for the wrapper reading, even the PT model has features in its top-20 differential list that are selective for harmful content: features labelled \texttt{knowledge\_semantics} like feature 329 (wrongdoing completion/intensification), feature 262 (concrete harmful-action verbs), feature 2184 (malicious intent / operational action), feature 8999 (deceptive framing), and feature 13571 (coercion and manipulation) all show significant differential activation on harmful prompts in the base model.

Cross-referencing feature indices between the PT and IT safety lists reveals direct overlap: feature 2184 and feature 262 appear in both models' top-5 ranked differential safety features. Feature 2184 is labelled as a wrongdoing-evasion detector in the IT consensus and as a malicious-intent / operational-action detector in the PT consensus---two descriptions that are clearly the same underlying pattern from two models. Feature 262 is similarly interpretable in both cases. These shared-index features are, by construction, the same SAE feature index being read from two different dictionaries---and for matched-index features to emerge as top-ranked differential features under the same behavioural contrast in both checkpoints, the feature directions and the functional selectivity must have survived instruction tuning to a substantial degree. Locating these features in the Subsection~\ref{subsec:feature-classification-threshold-robustness} classification places them in the modified class rather than the preserved class, which is the expected place for a feature whose behavioural role is retained but whose activation magnitude or distribution has shifted between checkpoints.

The safety contrast is therefore weaker than the instruction contrast but consistent with the same reading: the features that light up on harmful prompts in the IT model are substantially drawn from the same set as those in the PT model. Instruction tuning reweights and refines the selectivity of existing features rather than introducing a new safety-dedicated cluster from scratch. The reweighting is real (the 3-to-8 shift in explicit \texttt{safety\_alignment} labels is not noise) but it is a reweighting of which features dominate the top-20, not a categorical replacement. No feature in the IT top-20 safety list belongs to the IT-exclusive class; all have either a preserved or modified classification, meaning every one has at least some counterpart in the PT dictionary.

\end{figure}

\textbf{Cross-contrast observation.} Two feature indices, 262 and 2184, appear in the top differential list for both the safety contrast and (in the wider top-30) the instruction contrast, across both models. These features fire selectively on both harmful content and instruction-shaped prompts, consistent with much of the ``safety-relevant'' structure at layer 13 being encoded not as a dedicated safety signal but as an intersection of instruction-following structure and semantic content about wrongdoing. This is a non-trivial observation about how safety information is represented in Gemma 3 1B at layer 13 and reinforces the reading that instruction tuning modulates the combination of pre-existing features rather than installing a dedicated safety circuit.

In sum: the instruction contrast produces an essentially saturated wrapper-consistent result. The top differential instruction-style features in the IT model are overwhelmingly already present and selective in the base model; instruction tuning amplifies rather than creates them. The safety contrast is weaker but consistent: instruction tuning reweights which features dominate the top-20 toward more explicitly safety-aligned labels, but the features are substantially shared between checkpoints, and no IT-exclusive feature appears in the top-20 safety list. Both contrasts locate the behaviourally relevant features primarily in the modified class.

\subsection{Synthesis: What the Descriptive Evidence Says About the Wrapper Hypothesis}
\label{subsec:results-synthesis-wrapper-hypothesis}

The three strands of evidence converge on a consistent picture. The classification (Subsection~\ref{subsec:feature-classification-threshold-robustness}) shows that cleanly preserved features are a small minority, modified features dominate across the full threshold grid, and a substantial fraction of active features in both checkpoints lacks a strong counterpart in the other, though this last fact does not translate into behavioural novelty of the IT-exclusive class. The labelling (Subsection~\ref{subsec:automated-labelling-inter-annotator-agreement}) confirms that preserved features are overwhelmingly low-level linguistic rather than task-level, that instruction-style features are absent from the preserved class and concentrated in the modified class, and that the syntactic/instruction-style boundary is genuinely fuzzy even for capable annotators. The targeted analysis (Subsection~\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}) connects classification to behaviour: the features dominating the IT model's response to instruction-shaped prompts are largely the same indices dominating the PT model's response, with instruction tuning amplifying selectivity by an order of magnitude or more; and the features dominating the IT model's response to harmful prompts are substantially drawn from the same base-model population, with instruction tuning reweighting which features top the list rather than replacing them.

These findings are consistent with the wrapper hypothesis of \textcite{jain_mechanistically_2024} as originally formulated for procedurally defined tasks: the behaviours introduced by instruction tuning at layer 13 of Gemma 3 1B appear to be carried by reorganisation and reweighting of pre-existing features rather than creation of new ones. The strongest version (that no new features are created) is not supported: the IT-exclusive class is not empty. What is supported is the weaker claim that the features most behaviourally relevant to instruction-following and safety are not in the IT-exclusive class but in the modified class, whose members by construction have counterparts in the base model. The IT-exclusive class exists; it is just not where the behavioural action is.

Three caveats, developed in Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion}. First, the evidence is descriptive and correlational: it shows differential activation on behaviourally meaningful prompts but not that those features causally drive the behaviours. Second, the analysis covers a single middle layer of a single small model on a single corpus; none of these restrictions can be lifted from within the pipeline. Third, the labels depend on an automated six-way taxonomy whose boundaries are fuzzy (Subsection~\ref{subsec:automated-labelling-inter-annotator-agreement}), and \texttt{instruction\_style} and \texttt{safety\_alignment} labels are not ground truth. With those caveats, the evidence is sufficient to conclude that a wrapper-style reading of instruction tuning is consistent with feature-level data from a real instruction-tuned language model, and to motivate the questions about causal validation and generalisation discussed in Subsection~\ref{sec:future-work} and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}.
