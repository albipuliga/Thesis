\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Limitations and Robustness}
\label{sec:limitations-robustness}

A descriptive pipeline of the kind reported in Chapters 5 and 6 is only as trustworthy as its boundary conditions are clearly stated. This chapter sets out what the study did not do, what it could not do, and what it did do to check the robustness of the claims that Chapter 6 makes. It is organised into four sections that move from narrowest to broadest. Section 7.1 identifies the scope limitations that were fixed by design---the choice of a single model pair, a single layer, a single corpus, and a 5,000-sequence sample size---and discusses what conclusions those choices do and do not support. Section 7.2 turns to measurement limitations internal to the pipeline itself: the conditional-mean baseline used in the targeted analysis, the decoder unit-norm convention used by the Gemma Scope 2 SAEs and its consequences for amplitude-based comparisons, and the reliance on automated annotators with genuinely fuzzy taxonomic boundaries. Section 7.3 reports a small cross-SAE direction robustness check that tests whether the learned SAE decoder directions behave differently from random directions of matched magnitude; this is the demoted remnant of a larger activation-steering experiment whose full results did not support a clean behavioural interpretation, and this chapter explains both the check that remains and why the larger experiment is not reported as a primary result. Section 7.4 recaps the set of robustness checks that Chapter 6 does contain---threshold sensitivity, dual-annotator agreement, multiple behavioural contrasts, and the cross-SAE check of \S7.3---and argues that, taken together, these checks define a defensible envelope of confidence around the descriptive conclusions of the thesis.

\subsubsection{Scope Limitations}
\label{subsubsec:scope-limitations}

\textbf{Single model pair.} The entire pipeline operates on a single pair of checkpoints, the pre-trained and instruction-tuned variants of Gemma 3 1B, and on nothing else. This is a severe restriction on generalisation: nothing in Chapter 6 tests whether the wrapper-consistent reading of instruction tuning extends to larger models in the Gemma 3 family, to models in other families (Llama, Mistral, Qwen), or to fine-tuning regimes other than instruction tuning---such as reinforcement learning from human feedback \parencite{christiano_deep_2023, ouyang_training_2022}, constitutional AI \parencite{bai_constitutional_2022}, or direct preference optimisation \parencite{rafailov_direct_2024}. The single model pair is the unavoidable consequence of the matched-SAE requirement described in \S5.2: at the time this project was scoped, Gemma 3 1B was the only model for which publicly released Sparse Autoencoders existed for both a base and an instruction-tuned checkpoint at the same layer and width, and any generalisation to other models would require either training SAEs for those models or waiting for matched releases from the interpretability community. The results of Chapter 6 should therefore be read as a case study rather than as a general claim about instruction tuning, and the explicit framing of the research question in \S3.4 as a test of the wrapper hypothesis in a real instruction-tuned LLM at the feature level reflects this scoping.

\textbf{Single layer.} Residual-stream activations were collected only from layer 13, the midpoint of the 26-layer Gemma 3 1B architecture. The justification in \S5.1---that middle layers are where prior work on residual-stream SAEs reports the richest mixture of syntactic and semantic features---is defensible but not exhaustive. Layer 13 is a sampling point, not an optimum, and there is no a priori reason to expect the classification proportions, the labelling distributions, or the behavioural differentials of Chapter 6 to be layer-invariant. The likely directions of layer-dependence are worth noting. Earlier layers should shift the feature distribution toward low-level syntactic and tokenisation features and compress the semantic and instruction-style clusters; later layers should shift it in the opposite direction, toward more task- and output-oriented features. If the wrapper hypothesis holds layer-by-layer then the qualitative conclusion of Chapter 6---that the dominant mode of change is modification, and that behaviourally relevant features are shared between checkpoints---should also hold at other layers, but the quantitative proportions may differ considerably. A multi-layer replication is a natural and relatively cheap extension of the current pipeline, discussed in Chapter 8 as future work.

\textbf{Single corpus and probe-dataset choice.} The feature classification and labelling stages of the pipeline were run on 5,000 sequences from the \texttt{sample-10BT} split of FineWeb, a large-scale English web corpus \parencite{penedo_fineweb_2024}, while the targeted analysis used JailbreakBench, Stanford Alpaca \parencite{taori_stanford_alpaca_2023}, and TruthfulQA as behavioural probes. Both choices introduce their own distributional biases that interact with what the classification and labelling can detect. FineWeb is an English-only web text corpus and does not contain multilingual content, code, mathematical notation, or dialogue-style text in any significant fraction, which means that features specialised for any of these domains will appear as low-frequency or dormant in the activation statistics and will be filtered out by the 0.1\% active-feature cutoff. Similarly, the three probe datasets were chosen for coverage of distinct behavioural domains (safety, instruction-following, truthfulness) but are each a single dataset per domain, and the relative activation profiles reported in \S6.3 may not transfer to other safety benchmarks such as HarmBench, other instruction datasets such as Dolly or FLAN, or other truthfulness benchmarks. The corpus and probe-dataset choices should therefore be read as representative rather than exhaustive, and any strong claim about the generality of the Chapter 6 findings across domains is beyond what this pipeline can support.

\textbf{Corpus size.} The 5,000-sequence corpus was chosen after a pilot indicated that the key feature statistics---activation frequency, mean activation, and per-sequence mean activation vectors used in the activation-correlation step of \S5.4---stabilised well below 50,000 sequences. This empirical justification is sound for the classification stage, where what matters is the relative ordering of features and the stability of the per-feature statistics used to compute correlations. It is weaker for the labelling stage, where the top-20 activating contexts for each sampled feature are drawn from only the first 1,000 sequences of the corpus, and where a larger pool of activating contexts might sharpen the labels for rare features whose clearest examples were not among the first thousand sequences. It is weakest for the targeted analysis, where the probe datasets are much smaller (100 harmful prompts, 100 benign prompts, 200 Alpaca instructions, 200 TruthfulQA questions) and where the effective sample size for the Welch's $t$-tests is driven by the per-group prompt counts rather than by the size of the FineWeb pool. The per-group prompt counts of 100--200 are adequate for detecting the large effect sizes reported in \S6.3, where top-ranked features show ratios of $10\times$ to $140\times$ and $p$-values below machine floating-point precision, but would be marginal for detecting smaller effects. A study specifically targeting smaller-effect differential features would need a substantially larger probe set.

\subsubsection{Measurement Limitations}
\label{subsubsec:measurement-limitations}

\textbf{Conditional-mean baseline in the targeted analysis.} The group mean activation reported for each differential feature in \S6.3 is a conditional mean, computed only over the target group (e.g.\ harmful prompts) and contrasted against a conditional mean over the contrast group (e.g.\ benign prompts). It is not an unconditional mean over a full background distribution such as FineWeb. This is the correct choice for the purpose of the targeted analysis---identifying features that are selectively elevated on one group relative to another---but it has a consequence that must be stated explicitly: the raw activation numbers reported in \S6.3 are not absolute magnitudes, and cross-feature or cross-contrast comparisons based on those raw numbers are implicitly anchored to the conditional baseline. A feature with group mean 20 and contrast mean 2 is not necessarily ``more active overall'' than a feature with group mean 5 and contrast mean 0.05; the two ratios ($10\times$ and $100\times$) describe selectivity between the two groups, not overall activation magnitude, and reading the ratio as a magnitude statement would be a mistake. This clarification matters because an earlier draft of the pipeline was vulnerable to exactly this confusion, and because the distinction between conditional and unconditional baselines is the kind of subtlety that a careful reader of \S6.3 will reasonably ask about.

\textbf{Gemma Scope 2 decoder unit-norm convention.} The Gemma Scope 2 SAEs, in common with many other SAE releases, pre-normalise their decoder rows to unit $\ell_2$ norm before use. This convention is convenient for geometric comparisons---it is what makes the cosine similarity computation in \S5.4 a direct inner product without further normalisation---but it has a subtle consequence for any analysis that treats SAE feature directions as if they carried amplitude information. Specifically, because all decoder rows have the same norm, the ``strength'' of a feature in the residual stream is entirely encoded in its scalar encoder activation, with no contribution from the decoder side. This is not a problem for the \S5.4 classification or the \S5.6 labelling, both of which treat features as directions-plus-activations and compute all quantities accordingly. It \textit{is} a problem for any analysis that attempts to compare the ``natural'' injection magnitude of different features on a common scale, because a naive application of a fixed-multiplier intervention ($h \leftarrow h + \alpha d_i$) will produce sub-threshold perturbations for features whose typical encoder activation is much larger than $\alpha$ and over-large perturbations for features whose typical encoder activation is much smaller. The cross-SAE robustness check reported in \S7.3 addresses this specifically by using norm-calibrated multipliers based on empirical conditional mean activations---a detail whose importance is only legible against the backdrop of the unit-norm convention.

\textbf{Reliance on automated annotators.} The descriptive vocabulary attached to the classified features in \S6.2 comes entirely from two automated large language model annotators, Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.4-nano. The rationale for this choice is given in \S5.6: manual annotation of 200 features across two annotators would have been prohibitively time-consuming and would not have provided the inter-annotator reliability check that the dual-LLM setup gives for free. The cost of this choice is that the resulting labels are not ground truth. Specifically, the category assignments depend on a six-way taxonomy (\texttt{language\_syntax}, \texttt{knowledge\_semantics}, \texttt{instruction\_style}, \texttt{safety\_alignment}, \texttt{formatting}, \texttt{other}) that was fixed before running the labelling pass and whose boundaries turn out, as \S6.2 documents, to be genuinely fuzzy for both annotators in predictable ways---the \texttt{language\_syntax} $\leftrightarrow$ \texttt{instruction\_style} boundary alone accounts for a large fraction of all disagreement cases. The 60\% inter-annotator agreement rate reported in \S6.2 is a defensible lower bound on taxonomic stability but is not high enough to treat individual category labels as reliable for any single feature. The analyses of Chapter 6 therefore use categories distributionally---comparing category proportions across the four feature classes---rather than making any claim that rests on the category assignment of a single feature. This is a deliberate methodological choice, and the per-feature descriptions (the free-text summaries returned alongside the category labels) are used only qualitatively and illustratively in \S6.3.

\textbf{Active-feature threshold.} The 0.1\% activation-frequency cutoff used throughout Chapters 5 and 6 to exclude dead features is a free parameter that was not subjected to its own sensitivity grid. The choice of 0.1\% was made to remove features that essentially never activate on FineWeb, which would otherwise contribute noise to the activation-correlation computation and waste labelling budget if selected. A stricter cutoff would shrink the active set and bias the classification toward the most frequent features; a looser cutoff would expand it and admit rarely-active features whose statistics are dominated by noise. Informal checks during pipeline development suggested that the classification proportions are not strongly sensitive to the exact cutoff within the 0.05\%--0.5\% range, but no formal grid was computed. A full sensitivity analysis over the active-feature threshold alongside the $(\tau_d,\tau_a)$ grid of \S5.5 would be a straightforward extension.

\subsubsection{Cross-SAE Direction Robustness Check}
\label{subsubsec:cross-sae-direction-robustness-check}
\label{subsec:rq3-results}

The descriptive findings of Chapter 6 rely on two assumptions about the SAE decoder directions that are worth testing separately. The first is that these directions are not arbitrary---that they correspond to structured axes of variation in the residual stream rather than to any random unit vector one might select in the same space. The second is that the direction of an SAE decoder learned on one checkpoint retains some structural meaning when applied to the other checkpoint, which is what justifies the cross-model classification of \S5.4 in the first place. Both assumptions can be examined by a simple intervention experiment: injecting a real SAE decoder direction into the residual stream of a model and comparing the resulting distributional shift to the shift produced by injecting a random unit vector of the same magnitude into the same position. If the learned directions are structured in a way that matters for the model's computation, then real and random directions should produce measurably different effects.

The experiment was implemented as a small activation-steering procedure at layer 13. For each of a small set of test features drawn from the behaviourally differential lists of \S6.3, the SAE decoder row for that feature was scaled by a norm-calibrated multiplier---derived from the feature's empirical conditional mean activation on the relevant probe group, precisely to avoid the unit-norm amplitude problem discussed in \S7.2---and added to the residual stream of the target model during the forward pass on a set of probe prompts. The KL divergence between the output token distributions of the steered and unsteered runs was computed across a small batch of probe prompts. The same procedure was then repeated with a random unit vector in place of the real decoder direction, scaled to the same $\ell_2$ norm as the injected SAE direction, to produce a matched-norm random control. This random-control comparison is the robustness check: a feature whose decoder direction is structurally meaningful should produce a distributional shift that is systematically different from the shift produced by a random direction of the same magnitude, and the sign and magnitude of the difference is what this check measures.

The result, across multiple test features drawn from both Case A (IT-exclusive features injected into the base model) and Case B (PT-exclusive features injected into the instruction-tuned model), is consistent and statistically significant in a direction that was not anticipated by the experimental design. Real SAE decoder directions, when injected at matched norm, produce smaller KL shifts in the output distribution than random directions of the same norm, with the real-to-random ratio ranging from approximately 0.5 down to below 0.1 across test features and injection magnitudes, and with Welch's $t$-test $p$-values reaching below 0.001 for the clearest cases. In other words, the model's output distribution is more perturbed by a random perturbation in feature space than by a structured one of the same size. This is a significant result in the purely statistical sense (the real-versus-random gap is robust, repeatable, and large) but it does not admit the behavioural reading that a steering experiment would normally be designed to support. A steering result interpretable as evidence for or against the wrapper hypothesis would require the real direction to produce a larger, directional, and behaviourally interpretable shift than random, and none of these three conditions is met in the data.

The most natural interpretation of the real-versus-random gap is a structural one about the geometry of the learned SAE feature basis rather than a behavioural one about specific features. The model appears to ``absorb'' interventions along its own learned feature directions more gracefully than arbitrary perturbations---a property consistent with the intuition that these directions correspond to axes of variation the model already expects and has computational machinery prepared to handle. A random direction of the same magnitude is more disruptive precisely because it pushes the residual stream off the manifold of activations the model was trained to process. This reading is consistent with the broader observation that SAE features correspond to structured directions in residual-stream space rather than to arbitrary axes \parencite{cunningham_sparse_2023, park_linear_2024}, and it provides weak but non-trivial additional evidence that the SAE features used throughout Chapter 6 are not arbitrary constructs but correspond to something the model actually uses.

Two important limitations of this check must be stated explicitly. First, KL divergence is a blunt measurement: it captures that the output distribution has shifted but not how it has shifted, and in particular it cannot distinguish between shifts that align with the feature's semantic interpretation and shifts that merely disrupt the output in some unrelated way. A behaviourally grounded causal validation of specific features would require a task-specific readout---for example, the change in refusal rate on JailbreakBench prompts under steering of candidate safety features, or the change in instruction-following accuracy under steering of candidate instruction-style features---and this readout was not implemented in the current pipeline. Second, the check tests only the existence of a structural difference between real and random directions, not the direction or semantic meaning of that difference. The result is reported here as a weak structural observation and explicitly not interpreted as evidence for or against the wrapper hypothesis. The full-scale activation-steering experiment that would have provided a causal complement to the descriptive evidence of Chapter 6 is discussed in Chapter 8 as the single most important piece of future work.

\subsubsection{Summary of Checks Performed}
\label{subsubsec:summary-of-checks-performed}

Chapters 5 and 6 report four independent robustness mechanisms, each targeted at a different possible failure mode of the descriptive pipeline. These are collected here so that the reader can see them side-by-side rather than distributed across the methodology and results chapters.

First, the four-way classification of \S5.4 is subjected to a 25-point threshold sensitivity grid over $(\tau_d,\tau_a)\in\{0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9\}\times\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5,0.7\}$, reported in \S6.1 as three heatmaps and a numeric table in Appendix A. The qualitative shape of the classification---preserved features a small minority, modified features dominant, PT-exclusive and IT-exclusive classes sizeable but not behaviourally central---is robust across this grid, which is the direct evidence that the primary operating point $(0.7,0.3)$ is representative rather than cherry-picked.

Second, the descriptive vocabulary of \S6.2 is subjected to an inter-annotator reliability check via dual-LLM labelling with Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.4-nano, using identical prompts and identical six-way schemas. The resulting 60\% agreement rate and the structural concentration of disagreement at specific taxonomic boundaries constitute a form of rater-disagreement diagnostic that is itself reported as a finding, and every distributional claim in \S6.2 and \S6.3 is explicitly scoped to use category counts rather than individual feature labels in consequence of this check.

Third, the targeted behavioural analysis of \S5.7 and \S6.3 uses three distinct probe datasets---JailbreakBench for safety, Stanford Alpaca \parencite{taori_stanford_alpaca_2023} for instruction-following, and TruthfulQA for truthfulness---rather than a single contrast, and applies Welch's $t$-tests with an explicit $p < 0.01$ threshold and a minimum conditional-mean activation of 0.01 to filter noise-dominated features. The convergence of the safety and instruction contrasts on the same qualitative reading---that behaviourally differential features in the IT model are substantially shared with the PT model---is the direct cross-contrast robustness check.

Fourth, and reported in \S7.3 above, the cross-SAE direction robustness check compares the distributional effect of injecting real SAE decoder directions against matched-norm random controls, and finds a consistent and statistically significant structural gap that supports the interpretation of SAE features as non-arbitrary axes of variation in residual-stream space.

\textbf{Takeaway.} The descriptive conclusions of Chapter 6 are defended not by a single robustness check but by four: threshold stability, inter-annotator reliability, cross-contrast convergence, and real-versus-random structural distinctiveness. Each targets a different possible objection and each is reported with enough detail for a reader to reproduce it. None of the four can substitute for a behaviourally grounded causal experiment, and the absence of such an experiment is the most important limitation of this thesis---a limitation the pipeline cannot overcome from within and which is named as the primary direction of future work in Chapter 8.
