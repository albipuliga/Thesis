\section{Limitations and Robustness}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:limitations-robustness}

The trustworthiness of a descriptive pipeline is entirely dependent upon how well its boundary conditions have been articulated. In this chapter, we describe all things the research was unable to do or had no choice but to forego, all methods used to evaluate robustness, and order them from most specific to least. The first subsection~\ref{subsubsec:scope-limitations} defines the fixed scope limitations based upon design decisions: single model-pair, single layer, single corpus, 5,000 sequence sample. The second subsection~\ref{subsubsec:measurement-limitations} focuses upon internal measurements within the pipeline which can be considered measurement limitations. This includes the use of a conditional mean baseline in the targeted analyses; the unit-norm convention of decoder units, which has implications; and the reliance upon automated annotators with fuzzy taxonomic boundaries. The third subsection~\ref{subsubsec:cross-sae-direction-robustness-check} provides a description for a cross-SAE direction robustness test to determine whether the decoder directions that are used during training are statistically different from randomized directions of similar magnitude.. Although this is the remaining portion of a much broader experiment regarding steering activations, and although the original results did not allow for a clear behavioral interpretation, this section will discuss the successful test and explain why the remainder of the experiment cannot be presented as a primary finding. The fourth subsection~\ref{subsubsec:summary-of-checks-performed} will summarize those additional robustness checks contained in Chapter~\ref{sec:results}, and argue collectively that these define a reasonable envelope of confidence for accepting the descriptive findings of the present thesis.

\subsection{Scope Limitations}
\label{subsubsec:scope-limitations}

\textbf{Only Single Model Pair.} The pipeline works on one model-pair (PT and IT versions of Gemma 3 1B) and nothing else. None of the experiments described in Chapter~\ref{sec:results} investigate if the consistent-reading property of the wrapper can be extended to larger models in the Gemma 3 family, to different families of models (Llama, Mistral, Qwen), or to other training regimes such as RLHF \parencite{christiano_deep_2023, ouyang_training_2022}, constitutional AI \parencite{bai_constitutional_2022}, or direct preference optimisation \parencite{rafailov_direct_2024}. These restrictions follow directly from the constraint imposed by \S\ref{subsec:models-saes} (matched-SAE): at the time we scoped this project, the only publicly available SAEs for both a base and instruction-tuned checkpoint of the same layer and width were for the Gemma 3 1B. Therefore, generalization requires either training new SAEs for other models or waiting for SAEs from matched community releases. The results therefore represent a case study rather than a general claim regarding instruction tuning.

\textbf{Only One Layer.} Activation values have been gathered from only layer 13 of the 26-layer architecture. Although the reasoning in \S\ref{subsec:models-saes} is valid, it is not comprehensive. Layer 13 is a sampling point and represents neither an optimal layer nor necessarily a layer where the Chapter~\ref{sec:results} results could be expected to be invariant to layer. Early layers will likely produce a distribution of features centered around lower level syntax and tokenization features whereas late layers will likely produce a distribution centered around higher level task-specific and output-specific features. While there is no guarantee that the wrapper hypothesis will be layer-dependent, if so then the qualitative conclusions reached in Chapter~\ref{sec:results} (that modification predominates and behaviorally relevant features are shared across tasks) should also apply to all layers; however, quantitative proportions may differ.

\textbf{Only One Corpus and Only One Probe-Dataset Choice.} Classification has been performed using a 5,000 sequence FineWeb corpus \parencite{penedo_fineweb_2024} (\texttt{sample-10BT} split). Labelling context examples were selected from the first 1,000 sequence of that FineWeb corpus. Three probe-datasets have been employed in the targeted analysis (JailbreakBench, Stanford Alpaca \parencite{taori_stanford_alpaca_2023}, and TruthfulQA); both selection decisions introduced some form of bias due to their distributional properties. FineWeb is primarily composed of English language web text and contains virtually none of the multilingual content, code, mathematical notation, or dialogue common in significant fractions of other corpora; as a result, those specialized features will either appear in low frequencies or be ``dormant'' in comparison to the majority of the corpus and thus will be removed when applying a 0.1\% threshold filter. Additionally, while three datasets implement two distinct contrasts (the distinction between harmful vs.\ benign examples on JailbreakBench; instruction-shaped prompts from Alpaca compared to factual questions from TruthfulQA), they do not represent three separate probe-designs since each contrast-arm is sourced from only a single dataset; thus, the activation-profiles observed within \S\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results} cannot be assumed to generalize to other safety benchmark applications (e.g., HarmBench), instruction-based test sets (e.g., Dolly, FLAN), or alternative factual baselines than TruthfulQA.

\textbf{The Size of the Corpus.} A corpus consisting of 5,000 sequences was selected as a reasonable size based upon a pilot which demonstrated that the statistics of interest (activation frequency, mean activation, per-sequence mean activation vectors) stabilized below 50,000 sequences. The justification provided for classification applies because all that is required is that the relative ordering and stability of per-feature statistics be preserved. However, this justification is less applicable for labeling since top-20 activating contexts were selected from only the first 1,000 sequences of the 5,000 sequence FineWeb corpus; increasing the number of sequences from which examples were drawn may increase sharpness of labels for rare features. Finally, this justification is least applicable for targeted analysis since all probe datasets are significantly smaller (100 harmful, 100 benign, 200 Alpaca prompts, 200 TruthfulQA prompts) and effectively sampled-size for Welch's $t$-tests is determined by group-per-prompt count. While the counts per group in the range 100--200 are sufficient for producing large effect sizes in \S\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results} ($\sim$10$\times$ to $\sim$140$\times$ ratios, $p$-values $\ll$ machine precision), they are marginally sufficient for smaller effect sizes. To obtain smaller-effect sizes, would require a significantly larger probe-set.

\subsection{Measurement Limitations}
\label{subsubsec:measurement-limitations}

\textbf{Conditional-mean baseline in the targeted analysis.} The group mean activation for every differential feature in \S\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}, is a conditional mean calculated only for the target group (for example, harmful prompts) versus a conditional mean calculated only for the comparison group (benign prompts). The conditional mean, however, is not an unconditional mean calculated over the entire background distribution. While the use of the conditional mean is appropriate for identifying those features that are specifically enhanced on one group compared to another, it must be noted that the raw activation values presented in \S\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results} represent neither the total nor the average activation levels of those features. That is, a feature with a group mean value of 20 and a comparison mean value of 2 is not inherently ``more activated'' than a feature with a group mean value of 5 and a comparison mean value of $0.05$. Rather, their ratios ($10\times$ and $100\times$ respectively) reflect how selective they are regarding activity between groups, rather than how highly activated they are overall. In fact, prior to the current version of the pipeline being created, there existed some confusion around these two concepts, and therefore, it should come as little surprise that many knowledgeable readers of \S\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results} will pose questions regarding the difference between conditional and unconditional baselines.

\textbf{Gemma Scope 2 decoder unit-norm convention.} The Gemma Scope~2 SAEs \parencite{mcdougall_gemma_scope_2_2025} normalize decoder layers to unit $\ell_2$ norm. Normalizing decoder rows to unit $\ell_2$ norm is useful for making geometric comparisons (i.e., cosine similarity in \S\ref{subsec:feature-matching-classification} now represents an inner product) but can create issues when attempting to make amplitude-based analyses. Since the norms of all decoder rows are equal, a feature's strength in terms of residual output is completely determined by its scalar input to the encoder, and not at all by what comes out of the decoder. Therefore, nothing changes regarding \S\ref{subsec:feature-matching-classification} classification or \S\ref{subsec:automated-labelling} labeling since we continue to treat features as direction-plus-amplitude. However, if you were to attempt to compare ``naturally occurring'' input magnitudes across different features, then you would run into trouble. The reason is that any given multiplier-based intervention (such as $h \leftarrow h + \alpha d_i$) will result in very small perturbation sizes for features that normally receive large amplitudes and may cause over-large perturbations for features that typically receive very small amplitudes. The cross-SAE check performed in \S\ref{subsubsec:cross-sae-direction-robustness-check} addresses this problem by using norm-calibrated multipliers that are based on empirical conditional mean activations.

\textbf{Reliance on automated annotators.} The descriptors from \S\ref{subsec:automated-labelling-inter-annotator-agreement} were generated by two automatic LLM annotators. Therefore they come from an automatic LLM annotator named Claude Haiku 4.5 and another named GPT-5.4-nano. Manually adding descriptors to 200 different features would have taken too long. Also manual annotation would not have given the same level of inter-annotator reliability that we got automatically through using two LLMs. The trade-off is that the labels are not true labels. In order to do this the labels had to be assigned into a six-way category system that existed prior to doing the labeling. There are some fuzziness issues about the boundaries of these categories that exist for both annotators in very similar ways. For example, there is a big difference of opinion at the boundary between \texttt{language\_syntax} and \texttt{instruction\_style}. We feel that our 60\% agreement rate is a good lower bound on how much you can rely on the taxonomic boundaries. However it is low enough so that we cannot take each label as being individually correct. Therefore in chapter~\ref{sec:results} we use categories distributively (i.e., compare proportion distributions between categories), rather than making any claim based upon the assignment of any particular feature. As described in \S\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}, we only use the free-form description of each feature qualitatively.

\textbf{Active-feature threshold.} The 0.1\% activation frequency cut-off, used to eliminate dead features in the data, is an arbitrary number (a ``free'' parameter) that is not included in its own sensitivity grid. The reason for this is to remove features from the analysis that will rarely or never be activated by FineWeb, and thus add unnecessary ``noise'' to the correlation calculations and waste labeling money. If we were to tighten the cut-off (i.e.\ make the percent of time each feature activates lower), then we could potentially reduce the size of our active set, and bias towards those features that are most frequently activated. Conversely, if we loosen the cut-off (i.e.\ allow features to activate less often), then we may allow features that rarely activate to dominate their statistical measures due to the presence of ``noise''. As part of the informal checks performed during development indicated little variation in the classifications resulting from using different thresholds in the range of 0.05\%--0.5\%, no formal grid was constructed to test for such variations. It would be relatively simple to extend the sensitivity grid developed in \S\ref{subsec:threshold-selection} to include this threshold as a third dimension.

\subsection{Cross-SAE Direction Robustness Check}
\label{subsubsec:cross-sae-direction-robustness-check}

The Chapter~\ref{sec:results} results depend on two assumptions regarding SAE decoder directions. These assumptions need to be tested independently. First, that these decoder directions are not simply an arbitrary collection of vectors; rather they correspond to identifiable axes of variance within the residual streams. Second, assuming that a single decoder direction learned at a particular checkpoint has some inherent structure or meaning, that it will retain this structure when used to steer another. This assumption is the reason why we classify based upon differences between models (\S\ref{subsec:feature-matching-classification}). We can examine both by a simple intervention experiment: inject a ``real'' SAE decoder direction into a model's residual stream and measure the effect size of the resulting distributional shift relative to the size of the distributional shift produced by injecting a ``random'' unit vector of the same magnitude. There are significant differences in the ways in which learning directions may be structured as related to computations, so if the learned directions are indeed structured in such a manner, there should exist measurable differences between how real and random directions produce their respective distributional shifts.

We performed this experiment as an additional small activation steering experiment at layer 13. For each test feature selected from the differential list generated in \S\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}, we multiplied the SAE decoder row by a norm-calibrated factor (which was derived from the empirical conditional mean activation of the test feature on the relevant probe group, to circumvent the limitations associated with \S\ref{subsubsec:measurement-limitations}), and then added the result to the residual stream during a forward pass using all probes. Next, we calculated KL divergence of the steered output distribution relative to the unsteered output distribution over a small set of prompts. To generate a matched-norm random control for comparison, we replaced the ``real'' SAE decoder direction with a randomly chosen unit vector and scaled it to have the same $\ell_2$ norm as the ``real'' SAE decoder direction. A structurally meaningful decoder direction should produce a systematically different distributional shift than would be expected if the same amount of steering were provided by a random vector of similar magnitude.

The results of both Cases A (directions are inserted as exclusively IT-injected directions into the base model) and Case B (directions are exclusively PT-inserted into the IT-model) show the expected statistical significance that are consistent across all tests; however, they go in an opposite direction. When using a true SAE decoder direction at the same level of injection as randomized directions of the same norm, the KL difference (or shift) will be significantly \textit{smaller} when compared to randomly generated directions. The ratios of true vs.\ random were consistently around $0.50$ or less than $0.10$. Furthermore, Welch's $t$-test $p$-values showed values of $0.001$ or less for many of the most clear-cut examples. In other words, there was a statistically significant effect where the model produced larger changes (perturbations) due to a random change, than to a structure change of the same magnitude. This is clearly a significant effect (the gap is very large, repeatable, and robust); yet this cannot be interpreted as we typically do in experiments designed to study steering. To generate some kind of understandable evidence supporting the ``wrapper'' hypothesis would require a true direction producing a larger, directed, behaviorally meaningful shift than a random direction---which did not occur.

The best way to interpret this is as a structural explanation instead of an explanation based on behavior; the model ``takes in'' the interventions much better (i.e., less disruptively) in terms of the model's previously learned directions for the feature dimensionality than would be true if they were random or otherwise unrelated perturbations, which supports the idea that the directions represent axes of variation expected by the model and that the model was designed to handle. On the other hand, a random direction with equal magnitude would be more disruptive to the residual stream because it would move it off of the activation manifold where the model was trained to operate. This interpretation aligns with the observation that SAE features represent directional structure within the residual-space of the stream of residuals \parencite{cunningham_sparse_2023, park_linear_2024}. Furthermore, even though this is weak evidence, it also represents some form of non-trivial evidence supporting the notion that the SAE features utilized in Chapter~\ref{sec:results} do not simply represent arbitrary representations but have correspondence to something the model does use.

Two significant limitations of the current check. Firstly, KL divergence is a ``blunt'' metric; i.e., it will tell you if your target distribution has changed from your baseline distribution, but it doesn't give you information about what caused the change, nor does it tell you if the change happened along lines that are consistent with the intended semantic interpretation of the features being used, versus some other unconnected form of interference. In order to perform behaviourally-grounded causal validation of the wrapper hypothesis, we needed to implement a task-specific read-out (i.e., an objective measure of how performance changes when one uses the steering mechanism on JailbreakBench to steer the safety-feature, or on a follow-the-instruction task to steer the instruction-feature). However, such a read-out was not implemented. Secondly, this check only provides structural evidence of whether there exists a structural difference between the true direction and random direction, not semantic evidence regarding what these differences signify. Thus, our results represent a very weak structural observation and we do not interpret them as providing support either for or against the wrapper hypothesis. The full activation-steering experiment that would provide a causal complement to Chapter~\ref{sec:results} is discussed in Subsection~\ref{sec:future-work} as the most important future work.

\subsection{Summary of Checks Performed}
\label{subsubsec:summary-of-checks-performed}

Chapters~\ref{sec:methodology} and \ref{sec:results} report four independent robustness mechanisms, each targeting a different possible failure mode. They are collected here for convenience.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A 25-point threshold sensitivity grid} (\S\ref{subsec:feature-classification-threshold-robustness}). This confirms that the qualitative characteristics of the shape (preserved minority, changed dominant, exclusive classes large but not behaviorally central), were found to be stable throughout the entire grid. The primary operating point $(0.7, 0.3)$ was selected based upon its ability to represent the overall group and was not selected because it provided some unique insight into how the classifications varied.

    \item \textbf{An inter-annotator reliability check of labelling vocabulary} (Sections~\ref{subsec:automated-labelling-inter-annotator-agreement}--\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results}, Subsections~\ref{subsec:inter-annotator-reliability-in-label-vocabulary} and~\ref{subsec:inter-annotator-reliability-of-factual-cases}). Two LLMs (with identical prompts and schemas) generated labelling vocabularies. Agreement among raters resulted in a 60\% agreement rate. Additionally, the areas where disagreements occurred were concentrated at specific boundary points. As a result of these findings, all distributional claims made in Sections~\ref{subsec:automated-labelling-inter-annotator-agreement}--\ref{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis-results} utilized category counts rather than individual label data.

    \item \textbf{The targeted analysis.} Three probe datasets were used to test two behavioral contrasts---safety (JailbreakBench) and instruction-following (Alpaca \parencite{taori_stanford_alpaca_2023} vs.\ TruthfulQA as factual baseline). Welch's $t$-tests were used to compare differences between groups using $p < 0.01$ with a minimum activation filter applied. Because both contrasts produced similar qualitative readings, they provide evidence of cross-contrast robustness.

    \item \textbf{The cross-SAE direction check} (Subsubsection~\ref{subsubsec:cross-sae-direction-robustness-check}). This check determined that there exists a structurally significant difference between real directions and randomly generated directions. Therefore, SAE features are identified as non-arbitrary axes of variation.
\end{enumerate}

Each of the descriptive conclusions of Chapter~\ref{sec:results} are supported through four types of robustness checks. These include: threshold stability; inter-annotator reliability; cross-contrast convergence; and real-versus-random structural distinctiveness. Each type of robustness check addresses a different potential criticism. All robustness checks have sufficient detail so as to allow reproduction. While none substitute for a behaviorally grounded causal experiment, the absence of such an experiment is the most important limitation of this thesis, named as the primary future work in Section~\ref{sec:future-work}.
