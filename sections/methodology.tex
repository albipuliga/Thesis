\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This chapter describes the empirical pipeline used to test whether instruction tuning introduces new features into a language model's internal representations or predominantly reorganises pre-existing ones. The pipeline is organised as four stages that map directly onto the research question formulated in \S3.4. Stage one (\S5.1--5.3) fixes the model pair, the interpretability tool, and the activation corpus, ensuring that the pre-trained and instruction-tuned variants are compared in a shared geometric space using matched Sparse Autoencoders. Stage two (\S5.4--5.5) defines a feature-level correspondence between the two checkpoints based on two complementary measures---decoder direction similarity and activation correlation---and uses a grid over classification thresholds to check that the resulting four-way partition (preserved, modified, PT-exclusive, IT-exclusive) is not an artefact of any single threshold choice. Stage three (\S5.6) attaches a descriptive vocabulary to the sampled features through automated dual-LLM labelling with consensus construction, providing both the interpretive layer needed to discuss what individual features mean and a form of inter-annotator reliability check. Stage four (\S5.7) grounds the classification in behaviourally meaningful prompts by computing targeted differential activations across three contrast datasets---JailbreakBench, Stanford Alpaca, and TruthfulQA---using Welch's $t$-tests to identify features whose activation is selectively elevated on safety, instruction-following, or truthfulness probes. All code was implemented in a single Jupyter notebook using the SAELens and HuggingFace Transformers libraries; the full implementation and reproducibility artefacts are discussed in Chapter 6 alongside the results.

\subsection{Model Pair and Sparse Autoencoders}
\label{subsec:model-pair-saes}
\label{subsec:models-saes}

The experimental subject of this thesis is the Gemma 3 1B model family released by Google, specifically the pre-trained base checkpoint \texttt{google/gemma-3-1b-pt} and its instruction-tuned counterpart \texttt{google/gemma-3-1b-it}. Both checkpoints share the same architecture---26 transformer layers with a hidden size of 1152---and were loaded at \texttt{bfloat16} precision using the HuggingFace \texttt{transformers} library. The Gemma 3 family was chosen over larger alternatives for two complementary reasons. First, the 1B scale is small enough to fit entire pre-trained and instruction-tuned variants, their matched Sparse Autoencoders, and the activation buffers for a 5,000-sequence corpus into a single commodity GPU, which was a hard constraint for reproducibility and iteration speed. Second, and more importantly, this is the smallest scale at which publicly released Sparse Autoencoders exist for both a base and an instruction-tuned checkpoint of the same architecture, via the Gemma Scope 2 release \parencite{lieberum_gemma_2024}. This matching of SAEs to both variants is essential for the research question and is discussed further in \S5.2.

Sparse Autoencoders are the primary interpretability tool used throughout this thesis. Following the dictionary-learning framing of \parencite{bricken_monosemanticity_2023} and the formal setup of \textcite{cunningham_sparse_2023}, a Sparse Autoencoder learns an overcomplete dictionary of feature directions $\{d_i\}_{i=1}^{F}$ in the residual stream space of a target transformer layer, together with a sparse encoder that assigns each activation $h \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$ a non-negative feature vector $f(h) \in \mathbb{R}^{F}$ such that the reconstruction $\hat{h} = \sum_i f_i(h) \, d_i$ approximates $h$ in the mean-squared sense. The resulting feature directions are intended to correspond to semantically interpretable axes of variation, mitigating the superposition problem described by \textcite{elhage_toy_2022} whereby individual neurons participate in many unrelated computations. The specific SAEs used in this work come from the Gemma Scope 2 release: \texttt{gemma-scope-2-1b-pt-res} for the base model and \texttt{gemma-scope-2-1b-it-res} for the instruction-tuned model, both at the \texttt{layer\_13\_width\_16k\_l0\_medium} configuration. Both SAEs have a dictionary width of 16,384 features, use the JumpReLU encoder of \textcite{rajamanoharan_improving_2024}, and were trained by Google DeepMind at a comparable expected $L_0$ sparsity, making them directly comparable as feature dictionaries for the same underlying architecture.

Layer 13 was chosen as the target layer because it sits at the midpoint of the 26-layer stack, which is where prior work on Gemma Scope and on residual-stream SAEs more broadly has reported the richest mixture of syntactic and semantic features. Middle layers are generally understood to be where the abstract features relevant to downstream behaviour are most legible, whereas earlier layers are dominated by token-level and low-level syntactic features and later layers are increasingly output-oriented. A single-layer choice is a scoping decision rather than a methodological claim, and its implications for generalisation are discussed explicitly in Chapter 7.

\subsection{Why Gemma 3 1B and not Gemma 2 2B}
\label{subsec:why-gemma-3-1b}

An earlier version of this project planned to use the Gemma 2 2B pair, since at the time Gemma 2 was the default target of the original Gemma Scope release and of most downstream interpretability work. The migration to Gemma 3 1B was forced by a single but decisive consideration: at the time this project was scoped, the original Gemma Scope release provided publicly released SAEs only for the base \texttt{gemma-2-2b-pt} checkpoint, and no matched SAE existed for the instruction-tuned \texttt{gemma-2-2b-it} variant. The research question of this thesis---whether the same feature space is preserved, modified, or newly populated across checkpoints---is not answerable without SAEs trained on both variants, because there is no meaningful way to compare the feature dictionary of one checkpoint to a dictionary that does not exist for the other. Training an IT SAE ourselves was considered but ruled out on reproducibility and scope grounds: doing so would have introduced a large number of free hyperparameters whose choices would be impossible to validate against any reference, and the resulting dictionary would not be a recognised community artefact that other researchers could reuse. The Gemma Scope 2 release \parencite{lieberum_gemma_2024} resolved this problem by providing matched SAEs for both \texttt{gemma-3-1b-pt} and \texttt{gemma-3-1b-it} at identical layer and width configurations, and the project was migrated accordingly. One additional implementation detail is worth recording: Gemma 3 1B requires \texttt{bfloat16} rather than \texttt{float16} for inference, as the narrower dynamic range of \texttt{float16} produced overflow-driven NaN activations during early testing and would have silently corrupted the activation buffer. All model forward passes in this project therefore use \texttt{bfloat16}.

\subsection{Activation Collection}
\label{subsec:activation-collection}

The activation corpus consists of 5,000 sequences drawn from the HuggingFace \texttt{HuggingFaceFW/fineweb} dataset, specifically the \texttt{sample-10BT} split, accessed in streaming mode with a fixed random seed for reproducibility. Each sequence was tokenised with the shared Gemma tokenizer and truncated to exactly 256 tokens; sequences shorter than 256 tokens after tokenisation were discarded rather than padded, which guarantees that every retained sequence contributes the same number of activation rows and avoids the need to mask padding tokens during feature statistics. The 5,000-sequence figure was a deliberate downscaling from an initial 50,000-sequence plan, chosen after a pilot confirmed that the SAE feature statistics most important to the pipeline---per-feature activation frequency, mean activation, and per-sequence mean activation vectors used in the activation-correlation step of \S5.4---stabilised well below 50,000 sequences and that the additional data primarily increased runtime without changing the qualitative shape of the results. The final corpus contains approximately 1.275 million activation rows per model (5,000 sequences $\times$ 255 tokens after the first token of each sequence is dropped, as described below).

For each of the two models, activations were collected from the residual stream output of layer 13 using a PyTorch forward hook registered on the corresponding \texttt{model.model.layers[13]} module. Sequences were processed in batches of four, and within each batch the first token of each sequence was dropped before SAE encoding, because the first residual-stream position of a causal transformer carries no context from other tokens and its activation statistics are atypical. The resulting activation matrix was moved to CPU memory in \texttt{float32} and then re-encoded through the SAE in chunks of 64 rows at a time; this chunked encoding was necessary because naively encoding the entire activation matrix in a single GPU call caused out-of-memory errors on the 16,384-wide feature dictionary, and the one-sequence-at-a-time alternative was too slow to be practical. For each chunk, the SAE encoder, decoder, and reconstruction residual were computed, and three running statistics were updated per feature: a sum of activations, a sum of squared activations, and a count of non-zero activations. These running statistics were used after all batches to compute the per-feature mean activation $\bar{f}_i$ and activation frequency $\rho_i$. In parallel, a per-sequence mean activation vector was computed for each sequence, producing a matrix of shape $5000 \times 16384$ that was later used as the input to the activation-correlation step of \S5.4. Finally, two SAE quality metrics were tracked throughout encoding: the fraction of variance unexplained (FVU) of the reconstruction and the mean $L_0$ count, computed as the average number of non-zero features per activation row. These were reported at the end of each encoding pass as sanity checks that the pre-trained SAEs were operating within their expected regime on the FineWeb corpus rather than failing silently.

The end state of this phase is, for each of the two models, a complete summary of SAE feature behaviour over the corpus consisting of activation frequency, mean activation, and per-sequence mean activation vectors---in addition to the SAE decoder matrices themselves, which are loaded once and reused. These four objects are the only inputs required for the feature matching and classification stage described next.

\subsection{Feature Matching and Four-Way Classification}
\label{subsec:feature-matching-classification}

The central methodological construct of this thesis is the four-way classification of SAE features into preserved, modified, PT-exclusive, and IT-exclusive categories. Two complementary similarity measures are used to build this classification: decoder direction similarity, which is a purely geometric quantity computed from the SAE weights alone, and activation correlation, which is a functional quantity computed from how features co-activate on the shared FineWeb corpus. The rationale for using two measures rather than one is that either alone is insufficient. Decoder similarity is cheap to compute and captures whether the two dictionaries point in the same directions in residual-stream space, but it ignores whether the features with similar directions are actually used similarly by the two models. Activation correlation captures functional equivalence---whether a feature in the base model and a feature in the instruction-tuned model light up on the same sequences---but depends on the choice of similarity measure for matching features in the first place, and is dominated by noise for features that are rarely active. Using both measures jointly, and requiring agreement between them for the strongest category (preserved), is intended to produce a classification that is robust to the weaknesses of either measure in isolation.

Decoder direction similarity is computed as follows. Let $D^{\mathrm{PT}} \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times d}$ and $D^{\mathrm{IT}} \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times d}$ denote the decoder matrices of the PT and IT SAEs respectively, where $F = 16{,}384$ is the dictionary width and $d$ is the residual stream dimension. Both matrices are $\ell_2$-normalised row-wise so that every decoder direction is a unit vector, and the cosine similarity matrix is implicitly defined as $S = D^{\mathrm{PT}} (D^{\mathrm{IT}})^{\top}$. For each PT feature $i$, the best matching IT feature is found by taking $j^{*}(i) = \arg\max_j S_{ij}$ and its maximum cosine similarity is recorded as $s^{\mathrm{max}}_i$. The same procedure is run in the reverse direction to find, for each IT feature, the best matching PT feature. This two-directional matching matters because the best PT-to-IT match is not in general the same as the best IT-to-PT match, and the direction of matching affects which feature of each pair ``owns'' the comparison. In practice the computation is done in chunks of 1,024 rows to keep the intermediate similarity tensor within GPU memory limits, but this is a performance rather than methodological detail.

Activation correlation is computed over the per-sequence mean activation matrices collected in \S5.3. For each PT feature $i$ and its best-matched IT feature $j^{*}(i)$, the Pearson correlation of their per-sequence activation vectors is computed over the 5,000 sequences. A small numerical guard is applied to avoid division by zero on features whose per-sequence activation vector has zero variance (which occurs for features that either never activate or activate at exactly constant magnitude). The resulting scalar $\rho_i \in [-1,1]$ measures whether the PT feature and its geometric nearest neighbour in IT space are functionally correlated on the same inputs. The same quantity is computed in the reverse direction for IT features.

With these two measures in hand, the four-way classification at thresholds $(\tau_d,\tau_a)$ is defined as follows. A PT feature $i$ is called \textit{preserved} if both $s^{\mathrm{max}}_i \geq \tau_d$ and $\rho_i \geq \tau_a$, meaning that its best geometric match in IT space is both close in direction \textit{and} functionally correlated with it on the shared corpus. It is called \textit{modified} if exactly one of the two conditions holds---that is, if the direction is preserved but the function has drifted, or the function is preserved but the direction has rotated. It is called \textit{PT-exclusive} if neither condition holds, meaning that no strong geometric or functional counterpart exists in the IT dictionary. The fourth category, \textit{IT-exclusive}, is defined symmetrically on IT features using the reverse-direction best matches and their activation correlations, and identifies IT features with no strong counterpart in the PT dictionary. Together, the preserved, modified, and PT-exclusive classes partition the active PT features, while the IT-exclusive class covers the complementary set of active IT features; a feature is treated as \textit{active} if its activation frequency exceeds 0.1\% of tokens, a threshold intended to remove dead features from the analysis without being restrictive enough to bias the category distribution. The active-feature threshold is reported explicitly alongside the classification results in Chapter 6 so that readers can distinguish distributional findings from artefacts of feature pruning.

\subsection{Threshold Selection and Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{subsec:threshold-selection}

The classification described in \S5.4 depends on two free parameters, $\tau_d$ and $\tau_a$, and any single choice of these parameters would leave the results vulnerable to the criticism that the reported proportions of preserved, modified, and exclusive features are an artefact of a lucky threshold. To address this, the full classification is computed on a grid of 25 threshold pairs, with $\tau_d \in \{0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9\}$ and $\tau_a \in \{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5,0.7\}$. For each pair, the number of preserved, modified, PT-exclusive, and IT-exclusive features is recorded, together with the preservation rate (preserved / active PT) and modification rate (modified / active PT). The results are visualised as three heatmaps---preservation rate, modification rate, and PT-exclusive count---indexed by the threshold pair. The purpose of the grid is not to find an optimal threshold pair, since there is no ground truth against which ``optimal'' could be defined, but to confirm that the qualitative shape of the classification is stable across reasonable threshold choices.

The primary operating point used in the interpretive phases of the pipeline (\S5.6 and \S5.7) is $\tau_d=0.7$, $\tau_a=0.3$. This pair was selected \textit{after} the sensitivity grid was computed, on the following two grounds. First, it sits in the interior of the grid rather than at its boundary, which means that small perturbations in either threshold do not discontinuously change which features are assigned to which class. Second, it captures a moderate notion of both geometric and functional correspondence: $\tau_d=0.7$ is strict enough to exclude arbitrary near-random decoder alignments (for $d \sim 1000$ random unit vectors have expected cosine similarity close to zero, so 0.7 is well above chance) while being permissive enough to admit features whose directions have rotated mildly during fine-tuning, and $\tau_a=0.3$ is low enough to tolerate the substantial activation noise induced by the small sample size while still being well above the near-zero correlations expected for unrelated feature pairs. Stricter settings such as $\tau_d=0.8,\tau_a=0.7$ collapse the preserved set to a very small minority of features and inflate the exclusive categories, while more permissive settings wash out the distinction between modified and preserved. The chosen operating point is therefore a compromise that preserves the interpretive contrast without either of these failure modes, and the full grid reported in \S6.1 serves as the robustness check against this specific choice.

\subsection{Automated Feature Labelling and Consensus Construction}
\label{subsec:automated-labelling}

A bare four-way classification would be difficult to interpret without any description of what the classified features actually detect. To attach a descriptive vocabulary to the classification while keeping the interpretive effort manageable, this stage of the pipeline samples 50 features from each of the four classes and submits their top-activating contexts to two independent large language models---Claude Haiku 4.5 \parencite{anthropic_haiku_2025} and GPT-5.4-nano---for automated labelling. Using two models rather than one is a deliberate design choice: a single model's labels are difficult to audit, whereas two independent models provide a form of inter-annotator reliability check and make labeller disagreement a first-class observable that can itself be analysed.

Within each class, features are sampled with probability proportional to their activation frequency rather than uniformly. This choice weights the sample toward features that contribute meaningfully to the model's computation over the corpus, and avoids allocating labelling budget to rarely-active features whose top-activating examples are noisy and hard to interpret. For each sampled feature, the top 20 activating token contexts are collected by running the appropriate model and SAE over the first 1,000 sequences of the FineWeb corpus, extracting per-token feature activations, and keeping the highest-activating positions together with a window of 8 tokens on either side of the activating token. The activating token itself is marked with \texttt{>>>} and \texttt{<<<} delimiters in the extracted context so that the labelling model can unambiguously identify which token the feature fires on. This delimiter convention follows the display format used in the original Anthropic monosemanticity work and in subsequent SAE labelling pipelines.

Both labelling models receive identical prompts and identical output schemas, so that any differences in their outputs can be attributed to the models themselves rather than to prompt variation. The prompt instructs the model to act as a neural network interpretability expert, shows it the 20 top-activating contexts for a feature, and asks it to produce three outputs: a one-to-two sentence description of the pattern the feature detects, a single-label category drawn from a six-way taxonomy (\texttt{language\_syntax}, \texttt{knowledge\_semantics}, \texttt{instruction\_style}, \texttt{safety\_alignment}, \texttt{formatting}, \texttt{other}), and an integer confidence rating from 1 to 5. The output is constrained to this schema using the structured-output interfaces of the Anthropic and OpenAI SDKs, specifically \texttt{client.messages.parse} with a Pydantic \texttt{FeatureLabel} schema for Haiku and \texttt{client.beta.chat.completions.parse} with the equivalent schema for GPT. Structured outputs were essential for reliability: an earlier free-text version of the pipeline produced inconsistent category strings (``syntactic'', ``syntax'', ``grammar'', ``language'') that required ad-hoc normalisation, whereas the schema-constrained version produces categories that are directly comparable across models without post-processing. The labelling was run class-by-class, with a small rate-limit sleep between calls, and the resulting labels were serialised to disk as JSON after each class completed.

Consensus between the two labelling models is constructed as follows. For each sampled feature, if both models assign the same category, the feature is marked as an agreement case and that category becomes its consensus label. If the two models assign different categories, the feature is marked as a disagreement case, and the consensus label is taken from whichever model reported the higher confidence rating for that feature; in the event of equal confidence, Haiku is used as the primary model and GPT as the secondary. The consensus record for each feature additionally retains both descriptions and both confidence ratings, so that disagreement cases can be inspected qualitatively rather than only counted. The resulting consensus labels support two distinct uses in Chapter 6: first, the agreement rate and disagreement confusion matrix serve as an inter-annotator reliability diagnostic and give a measurable lower bound on the stability of the six-way taxonomy; second, within the agreement set, the distribution of categories across the four feature classes provides the descriptive evidence used to discuss what kinds of features are preserved, modified, or exclusive to each checkpoint. Both uses are discussed as results in \S6.2.

\subsection{Targeted Differential Analysis on Behavioural Probes}
\label{subsec:targeted-differential-analysis}

The labelling stage attaches descriptive vocabulary to a sample of features but does not by itself show that any of those features matters for the behaviours that instruction tuning is supposed to induce. To connect the feature-level classification to behaviourally meaningful domains, a targeted differential analysis is run on three external probe datasets: JailbreakBench \parencite{chao_jailbreakbench_2024} for safety-relevant prompts, the Stanford Alpaca instruction dataset \parencite{taori_stanford_alpaca_2023} for instruction-following prompts, and TruthfulQA \parencite{lin_truthfulqa_2022} for truthfulness probes. The design of the contrast is important. For safety, the harmful-split prompts of JailbreakBench are contrasted against the benign-split prompts of the same dataset rather than against generic FineWeb text, so that the differential activation isolates safety-relevant structure rather than any incidental stylistic difference between task-oriented prompts and web text. For instructions, the first 200 Alpaca instructions are contrasted against the first 200 TruthfulQA questions, providing a contrast between imperatively-phrased task prompts and interrogatively-phrased factual queries. Truthfulness itself is used both as a probe contrast against instructions and as part of the baseline for the safety comparison, giving a three-way design over four prompt pools (harmful, benign, instruction, truthful).

For each of the three contrasts and each of the two models, SAE feature activations are computed on every probe prompt using the same activation collection procedure as in \S5.3, but with a shorter maximum sequence length of 128 tokens appropriate to the probe prompts and with per-prompt rather than per-sequence feature statistics. The per-prompt activation of a feature is defined as the mean of its token-level activations over all non-first tokens of the prompt, and is stored as a row in a prompts $\times$ features matrix per model. Two such matrices are produced per model---one for the safety-vs-benign contrast and one for the instruction-vs-truthful contrast---and all subsequent analysis is performed feature-by-feature on these matrices.

For each feature, a Welch's two-sample $t$-test \parencite{welch_generalization_1947} is run comparing its per-prompt activations on the target group (e.g.\ harmful prompts) to those on the contrast group (e.g.\ benign prompts). Welch's variant is used rather than the standard equal-variance $t$-test because the two groups cannot be assumed to have equal variance---a feature that is selectively active on harmful prompts is, almost by definition, more variable on the group where it activates than on the group where it does not. The implementation uses \texttt{scipy.stats.ttest\_ind} with \texttt{equal\_var=False}. Features are retained as differential if they satisfy two conditions simultaneously: a $t$-test $p$-value below 0.01 and a target-group mean activation above 0.01, the latter being a noise-floor filter that prevents the analysis from being dominated by features that are statistically elevated only because their baseline activation is essentially zero. For each retained feature the pipeline records its feature index, target-group and contrast-group mean activations, their difference and ratio, and the test statistic and $p$-value. The top 30 differential features per contrast per model are then ranked by difference and carried forward to the labelling pipeline of \S5.6, which re-labels them on examples drawn from the probe prompts themselves rather than from FineWeb. This re-labelling step produces descriptions that are grounded in the behavioural contrast rather than in generic web text, and enables the results of \S6.3 to discuss not only which features are differential but what they appear to detect.

A design note that matters for interpretation: the group mean activation reported for each differential feature is a conditional mean, computed only over prompts in the target group, and not an unconditional mean over the full prompt pool or the full FineWeb corpus. This distinction is subtle but consequential, because it means that comparisons across features or across contrasts based on the raw group-mean number reflect conditional rather than absolute activation strength. This is the appropriate choice for identifying features selective for a given behavioural domain, since what matters is whether the feature lights up more on the target than on the contrast, but it must be stated explicitly when the numbers are reported in Chapter 6 to avoid implying an unconditional baseline that was never measured. The robustness and limitations section of Chapter 7 returns to this point as part of the broader discussion of measurement choices.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:methodology-summary}

The four stages of the pipeline described in this chapter together produce three artefacts that feed directly into the results and discussion of Chapters 6 and 7. The classification stage yields a four-way partition of the active SAE features in each checkpoint, together with a 25-point threshold sensitivity grid that quantifies the stability of the partition. The labelling stage yields consensus descriptive labels for 200 sampled features together with an explicit inter-annotator agreement statistic and a disagreement confusion matrix. The targeted stage yields three ranked lists of differential features---safety, instruction, and truthfulness---each annotated with Welch's $t$-test statistics and with their classification status in the original four-way partition, so that the two analyses can be cross-referenced at the feature level. Together these three artefacts are designed to let Chapter 6 address the research question of \S3.4 in descriptive, distributional, and behaviourally grounded terms, without committing to causal claims that the measurement setup of this pipeline cannot support.
