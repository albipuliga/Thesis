\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Problem and Motivation}
\label{sec:problem-motivation}

All the modern large language models (LLMs) being utilized by millions of users today have had to go through extensive post-training pipeline modifications that involve layered supervised instruction tuning, preference learning from user feedback, Constitutional AI, and direct preference optimization on top of a base model that was initially trained to predict the next token based on Web-Scale Text \parencite{christiano_deep_2023, ouyang_training_2022, ziegler_fine-tuning_2020, bai_constitutional_2022, rafailov_direct_2024}. It is primarily the post-training component, rather than the base model, that gives the deployed system its characteristic behaviours such as following the given instructions, responding using a certain tone/register, rejecting dangerous/inappropriate requests, and maintaining the role of an assistant. However, the majority of mechanistic interpretability research has focused on understanding base models. From \textcite{olah_zoom_2020}'s Zoom In article concerning circuits, to the indirect object identification circuit of \textcite{wang_interpretability_2022}, to the factual association edits of \textcite{meng_locating_2023}, and the feed forward key-value memory of \textcite{geva_transformer_2021}. Consequently, there is a gap between the layer of the language model that interpretability understands best and the one that users experience.

There are many reasons why this gap is important beyond just providing a complete picture. For example, without knowing how instruction tuning affects a model at a representational level we will never be able to determine whether a behavior exhibited by a deployed system represents a true property of the model or if it is simply an artifact created during post-training that would be easily eliminated through additional fine-tuning, could be "jail broken" via adversarial prompt engineering, or even circumvented by deceptive alignment \parencite{zou_universal_2023, hubinger_sleeper_2024}. Currently our ability to address these types of questions relies heavily upon behavioral analysis (probe the deployed system, observe whether it rejects dangerous/prompts, etc.) and therefore, an inside-the-box representation analysis of what internal representations post-training introduces/uses/reconfigures is missing.


A widely cited position in regards to the mechanism of Instruction Tuning is the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis presented by \textcite{zhou_lima_2023}: surprisingly little amounts of high quality instructional data are required to generate strong instruction following behaviour, suggesting that Instruction Tuning may not create new representations but instead teaches a model to access pre-existing ones. An opposing position that is less frequently referenced but is implied in much of the alignment literature suggests that Instruction Tuning creates new computational structures (features/circuits/determination processes) that did not previously exist in the base model. Both positions imply that Instruction Tuning creates different representations within the model and that distinguishing between them requires determining whether a specific feature present in the Instruction-Tuned Model's Layer 13 Residual Stream has a corresponding feature in the Base Model's Layer 13 Residual Stream.

Until relatively recently, addressing this difference at a representation level was practically unfeasible. The primary mechanisms available for performing mechanistic interpretability (classifier probing, activation patching, attention pattern inspection, residual stream decomposition) were either designed to measure overall behavioral properties of a model or focus on analyzing single circuits at a time; none of these mechanisms provided an approach for directly comparing the entire representation state of two models.

Sparse Autoencoders resolved this issue. Initially explored in terms of toy-model superposition (\textcite{elhage_toy_2022}) and early results on monosemantics (\parencite{cunningham_sparse_2023}), sparse autoencoders now enable the creation of feature dictionaries describing residual-stream activations of a model. Each entry in the dictionary describes a vector pointing along a dimension of activation space tending to activate along semantically interpretable patterns. As such, two checkpoints representing the same model prior to and subsequent to post-training can be equipped with sparse autoencoders trained on the same residual-stream locations and have their feature dictionaries compared directly. A feature represented in both dictionaries that point in the same direction and activate on the same input has been preserved throughout post-training. A feature represented in only one dictionary or whose direction or functional behavior has been altered was modified, added or deleted. This type of comparison operationalizes the empirical dispute over whether Instruction Tuning creates new capabilities versus merely aligning existing capabilities towards instructional tasks. This is precisely what this thesis aims to investigate.

Why Study Instruction Tuning Specifically? Practically, instruction tuning is the first and least complex stage in post-training; therefore its effect can be separated from those resulting from subsequent reinforcement learning-based stages, provided that matched Sparse Autoencoders exist for both the base model and the supervision-fine-tuned checkpoint. The Gemma Scope 2 Release (\parencite{mcdougall_gemma_scope_2_2025}) provides matching Sparse Autoencoders for both the Gemma 3 1B base and Instruction-Tuned versions; this constraint ultimately determined the selection of model for this thesis (See Subsection~\ref{subsec:models-saes}). Scientifically, Instruction Tuning is also the stage whose mechanism is most directly investigated by the superficial-alignment literature and very recent mechanistic fine-tuning work (\parencite{jain_mechanistically_2024, prakash_fine-tuning_2024, du_how_2025}); thus, a feature-level investigation enables a connection to an ongoing debate rather than initiating one entirely.

\subsubsection{Representational Foundations: Superposition, Linear Features, and the Case for Dictionary Learning}
\label{subsubsec:repr-foundations}

It has been acknowledged that the unit-of-analysis issue in mechanistic interpretability has existed prior to the actual formation of the mechanistic interpretability area; i.e., the issue had already arisen in research on distributed word representations \parencite{mikolov_efficient_2013} which demonstrated that directions within an embedding space exhibit semantically meaningful structure which is uninterpretable based upon any one of the individual coordinates. Individual neurons are not the proper unit of analysis since each neuron contributes to many unrelated computations. \textcite{radford_learning_2017} illustrated this concept using character-level language models. Although Radford et al. Found that there were exceptions, such as a "sentiment neuron", they noted that sentiment neurons would not typically be the norm. \textcite{olah_feature_2017, olah_zoom_2020} developed the overarching viewpoint that the appropriate unit of analysis should be the feature, a direction within activation space representing semantically legible patterns, which may or may not correlate with a particular neuron.

There exists a formal explanation that neurons do not constitute the proper unit of analysis due to \emph{superposition} defined by \textcite{elhage_toy_2022}: a network containing n number of neurons can only provide n orthogonal directions exactly; however, a network can provide many more than n near-orthogonal directions approximately if those near-orthogonal directions are sparse (inactive on most inputs). A sparse set of features can be compressed into a lower-dimensional space with tolerable interference. As a result, the representation provides each feature with a direction that is a linear combination of several neurons. Therefore, single-neuron interpretation is not only aesthetically limited but also methodologically incorrect when examining overcomplete learned representations: the neurons will be polysemantic by design. \textcite{scherlis_polysemanticity_2025} further extends this analysis by developing a more rigorous account of polysemanticity as a function of capacity; \textcite{park_linear_2024} also develops the linear representation hypothesis, that meaningful features correspond to directions rather than nonlinear manifolds as structural claims about transformer internals, which can be tested.

Superposition motivates dictionary learning as the appropriate remedial action. If true representational units are features residing as sparse linear combinations of neurons, then the correct method for recovering them would be to learn a dictionary of feature directions and an encoder which assigns each activation a sparse code over said dictionary. The above idea is classical within signal processing. It was first demonstrated with language models using dictionary learning applied to neural networks in \textcite{cunningham_sparse_2023} who showed that dictionaries trained on sparsely activated transformer activations provided substantially more interpretable features than were obtainable through single-neuron interpretation. Simultaneous work in Anthropic's Towards Monosemanticity line \parencite{bricken_monosemanticity_2023} further developed the approach to larger transformers and established much of the vocabulary ("features," "monosemantic directions," "activating examples") now standard in the SAE literature. This thesis relies upon the premise that features recovered through dictionary learning on a transformer's residual stream are meaningful representational units and that two transformers whose residual streams have been decoded in terms of dictionary learning in the same manner can be compared at the level of their features

\subsubsection{Sparse Autoencoders as an Interpretability Tool}
\label{subsubsec:saes-tool}

Early SAE research applied a form of the $L_1$ sparsity penalty to the output of the encoder, but it is well-documented that this penalty has well-known failure modes. These include the fact that when applying an $L_1$ penalty to active features, those features will be shrunk towards zero, thereby introducing a strong bias into both the reconstruction process as well as downstream analyses. In addition, because the sparsity regimes in early SAE formulations were fixed, tuning such regimes across different layers and/or different scales can be extremely challenging. Therefore two improvements have been developed. 
\textcite{rajamanoharan_improving_2024} showed that by creating gated SAE's one can disentangle the decision of whether or not to activate a particular feature from how much to activate that feature, thus greatly decreasing the degree to which the $L_1$ penalty shrinks the value of active features. Later, \textcite{gao_scaling_2024} found empirical scaling laws relating SAE performance as a function of three parameters: dictionary width, training compute and sparsity target size, that guided many of the subsequent SAE releases.
Additionally, \textcite{karvonen_saebench_2025} created SAEBench, an evaluation benchmark that measures the quality of SAEs using more than just reconstruction loss (as measured via feature interpretability, probe-based downstream utility, etc., as well as robustness to perturbations).

The most relevant artefact for this thesis is the Gemma Scope~2 technical report and the associated matched-SAE release for Gemma~3 \parencite{mcdougall_gemma_scope_2_2025}. The original Gemma Scope suite included trained Sparse Autoencoders for every layer and several widths on Gemma~2 \parencite{lieberum_gemma_2024}; the documentation of Gemma Scope~2 described the paired base and instruction-tuned SAEs for Gemma~3, including the dictionaries that are being utilized. Due to the scale and reproducibility of the Gemma Scope releases it became possible for researchers to assume there existed high-quality trained SAEs for use with a production class model, whereas before these releases, researchers would need to train their own. \textcite{peng_use_2025} states that SAEs are most beneficial for discovering new concepts rather than utilizing pre-existing knowledge of those concepts, which provides additional insight into the nature of the Targeted Analysis Stage of this thesis, where behavioral probe datasets allow the feature space to create its own concepts and do not require researcher-defined concepts.

\subsubsection{Post-Training and Its Effects on Internal Representations}
\label{subsubsec:post-training-effects}

Post-training consists of all stages and processes that a language model undergoes after pre-training on next-token prediction. The typical pipeline begins with Supervised Instruction Tuning \parencite{wei_finetuned_2022, zhou_lima_2023}; followed by Reward Modeling \parencite{christiano_deep_2023, ziegler_fine-tuning_2020}; and then concluding with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback \parencite{ouyang_training_2022}. Constitutional AI \parencite{bai_constitutional_2022}, and other similar approaches substitute or add model-generated feedback based on explicit principles in place of human feedback, and Direct Preference Optimization (\textcite{rafailov_direct_2024}) transforms the RL phase into a direct optimization problem, which circumvents the need for a reward model. The more general Helpful-and-Harmless perspective in \textcite{bai_training_2022} defines the objective as developing a model that is both useful and safe.

Previous research in the interpretability literature of how these actions affect the model's internal representations comes from three different perspectives. The research question of this dissertation exists at their intersection.

\textbf{The Superficial Alignment Argument.} The most robust expression of this view is LIMA \parencite{zhou_lima_2023}, where the principal result is that 1000 hand-crafted instruction-response pairs can be used to generate high-quality instruction-following behavior in a large base model, comparable to models fine-tuned over many orders-of-magnitude greater amounts of data. Their own interpretation is clear: a model's knowledge and abilities are acquired almost completely through pre-training, while alignment simply tells the model which subset of formats to draw upon when interacting with users. This is an argument supported by behavioral evidence, and does not imply any specific mechanism of representation, however it makes a clear prediction regarding what an interpretability study would find: that the substrate for instruction-following behavior is present in the base model prior to fine-tuning; and fine-tuning only changes the features that are read out and in what register, rather than creating new ones. LIMA does not test this prediction at the representational level; but frames the empirical question answered by this dissertation.

\textbf{Mechanistic Fine-Tuning Line.} In parallel, mechanistic research uses circuit-level and probe-level tools to look into what structures inside the model change when it is fine-tuned. The foundational piece of literature for this dissertation is \textcite{jain_mechanistically_2024}, which studies the fine-tuning process in controlled synthetic contexts through the use of compiled Tracr transformer models and probabilistic context-free grammars. In particular, it demonstrates that the application of fine-tuning rarely results in changes to the underlying capabilities of a model. Rather, the process typically identifies a "minimal transformation" or "wrapper," on top of these capabilities. The wrapper hypothesis (the idea that fine-tuning instills a minimal re-routed version of the existing capabilities rather than creating entirely new capabilities) is the claim tested in this dissertation in a novel context. While Jain et al.'s support their findings through the use of pruning, probing, and reverse fine-tuning in synthetic environments with known ground truth, they do so without conducting feature-level analysis based upon SAE or utilizing a production-quality model. Therefore, extending their findings to the feature-level analysis conducted with a production-quality instruction-tuned LLM is the extension made in this study.

Immediately adjacent to the study conducted by Jain et al.'s, \textcite{prakash_fine-tuning_2024} utilizes similar circuit-level tools to investigate how well fine-tuning for entity tracking actually modifies the behavior of the base model at the circuit level. Specifically, it demonstrates that fine-tuning does not create a new circuit within the model; however, it does enhance both the selectivity and magnitude of a single circuit that exists in the base model. \textcite{du_how_2025}  builds upon this understanding of how fine-tuning affects circuits and seeks to determine which elements of a model's behavior are most significantly impacted by post-training at the representational level. Finally, \textcite{xu_tracking_nodate} investigates the mechanisms underlying pre-training itself at a finer granularity and provides the additional insight that feature-level analysis can be utilized to monitor representational changes across training, providing a temporal analog to the checkpoint comparisons provided in this dissertation.

\textbf{Representation engineering and the causal aspect.} A third area of literature considers representations both as subjects of interpretation and as objects of manipulation. \textcite{zou_representation_2025} proposed a top-down strategy to extract concept directions from model activation by way of contrasting pairs and utilize those to observe and control behavior. They demonstrated that through simple linear transformations, one could interpret properties such as honesty, refusal and emotional valence from the residual stream, and manipulate them into it. Therefore, this represents the causal analog to correlational, feature-level analyses of SAE. While the literature surrounding representation engineering serves as background for this dissertation, its empirical contribution will remain limited to description and correlation, with causality being addressed in behavioral terms in subsequent work (discussed in Subsection~\ref{sec:future-work}).

The motivation behind the jailbreak and adversarial attack literature (\parencite{zou_universal_2023, hubinger_sleeper_2024}) comes next. If safety behaviors are just a wrapper over the capabilities of a base model, then adversarial prompts that bypass the safety mechanisms should surface capabilities the instruction-tuned model would otherwise not have. A feature-level description of which representations are shared between the two checkpoints is directly relevant to understanding where such surfacing can and cannot reach.

\textbf{Similarity-of-representations work.} One further adjacent piece:\textcite{kornblith_similarity_2019} developed Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) and related similarity metrics for comparing neural network representations. CKA computes a global metric that quantifies the similarity between representations produced by two neural networks at a particular layer. It has been applied widely to compare the representations produced by fine-tuned models relative to those of their respective base models. Aggregate similarity is complementary to the feature-level comparison of this research. CKA measures how different two representations are globally, while a feature-level comparison identifies specifically which dimensions are different and to what extent. This dissertation did not calculate CKA between the PT and IT residual streams; however, if desired, the feature level results from this study could potentially be checked against results obtained from calculating such a global similarity in future studies that replicate the findings presented within this dissertation.

\subsubsection{The Gap and the Research Question}
\label{subsubsec:gap-rq}

Literature surveyed in Subsubsections~\ref{subsubsec:repr-foundations} through \ref{subsubsec:post-training-effects}, indicates that the three major points that have emerged from this body of literature are: (i) sparse autoencoders have developed to a point where they provide a feature-level lexicon for the residual portion of a production-grade model; (ii) there exists matched SAEs for the Gemma 3 1B base and instruction-tuned checkpoint versions by way of Gemma Scope~2 \parencite{mcdougall_gemma_scope_2_2025}; and (iii), there is an empirical hypothesis which suggests that instruction tuning largely re-exposes previously installed base model capabilities instead of adding entirely new ones, notably expressed in LIMA and implied throughout much of the alignment literature. Further, this hypothesis has been empirically tested with other non-SAE-based tools in artificial environments and with circuit-level based tools in task-specific environments. The results have generally supported this hypothesis in each case. However, this hypothesis has not yet been examined using feature-level Sparse Autoencoders on actual instruction-tuned LLMs.

This thesis bridges the gap established by these findings: no previous research has used matched Sparse Autoencoders to examine the feature-level representations of a real pre-trained and instruction-tuned language model at the exact same layer, nor has it investigated whether the behaviourally relevant features of the instruction-tuned version are maintained, modified or completely novel compared to its base model. The ability to do so was made possible by the Gemma Scope~2 release \parencite{mcdougall_gemma_scope_2_2025} regarding the comparison of the pre-trained and instruction-tuned variants of Gemma 3 1B, and therefore motivated the pipeline outlined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

The research question: at the feature level, does instruction tuning add new features to a language models' internal representations, or are they mainly reorganisation of previously present ones? More specifically, if matched Sparse Autoencoders are applied to decode the residual streams of a pre-trained and an instruction-tuned variant of Gemma 3 1B at layer 13, are the features which are most important to understanding the performance of a language model in terms of its ability to follow instructions and safety related prompts best described as being preserved, modified or unique to one of the two checkpoints?

This is a narrower question than the entire superficial alignment vs. new capability debate. It limits itself to a single layer of a single model pair, a single post-training phase and an explicitly descriptive/correlational approach.

This study will be operationalized by two sub-questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item RQ1: The percentage of characteristics in Layer 13 of Gemma 3 1B that were kept, changed, PT-exclusive or IT-exclusive when comparing the base checkpoint to the IT checkpoint; and how robust is that percentage to changes in matching thresholds?
    \item RQ2: When the IT model is probed with safety-relevant and instruction-following prompts, do the features with the greatest differential activity correspond to IT-exclusive entries according to a strict creation interpretation, or to those features matched to an existing PT entry in the pipeline?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Main Contribution}
\label{sec:main-contribution}

This thesis provides a four-stage approach to investigate the research questions. A specific example of this pipeline is demonstrated for both the pre-trained version and the instruction-tuned version of Gemma 3 1B at layer 13 residual stream using the \mbox{\texttt{gemma-scope-2-1b-pt-res}} and \mbox{\texttt{gemma-scope-2-1b-it-res}} SAEs from Gemma Scope~2 \parencite{mcdougall_gemma_scope_2_2025}.

The concrete deliverables are the following:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The pipeline itself.} Four loosely-coupled stages: Activation Collection, Feature Matching Across Checkpoints, Dual-LLM Consensus Labelling, and Targeted Differential Activation Analysis, all described in Section ~\ref{sec:methodology}. This can serve as a protocol and template that can be reused as matched SAEs become available for additional model pairs, to further this research to other models and post-training phases.

    \item \textbf{Empirical findings on Gemma 3 1B.} The Pipeline produced empirical evidence indicating that the features found to be most behaviorally relevant to instruction-following and safety related prompts were primarily reorganized preexisting features versus new features; this is a descriptive finding consistent with the Wrapper Hypothesis of \textcite{jain_mechanistically_2024}. All findings from the pipeline appear in Chapter ~\ref{sec:results}, and the reader can expect to find a detailed view where features that remain unchanged are a relatively small portion, the modified Class Dominates, and Instruction Type Features are concentrated within the Modified Class vs. IT Exclusive Class.    
\end{enumerate}
